The Phases of the Moon

Half the Moon is lit at every moment of every night. The phases are not something happening to the Moon — they are what that lit half looks like from a vantage point that keeps moving. Here is the geometry, and what it puts in the sky over Exeter.

The setupNothing is casting a shadow

The most common explanation of the phases — that the Earth's shadow falls across the Moon — describes something real, but rare. That is a lunar eclipse, and it happens two or three times a year at most. The phases happen every month, in daylight as well as at night, and they have nothing to do with shadows at all.

The Sun lights one hemisphere of the Moon, always, the same way it lights one hemisphere of the Earth. What changes over a month is not the lighting but our angle onto it. The Moon takes about 29.5 days to go once around the Earth relative to the Sun, and as it goes we see the lit hemisphere from the side, then from behind, then from the side again.

The single number that decides everything

Elongation is the angle Sun–Earth–Moon: how far around the sky the Moon has drifted from the Sun. At 0° the Moon is between us and the Sun and we are looking at its unlit side — a new moon. At 180° the Moon is opposite the Sun and the lit hemisphere faces us squarely — a full moon. Every phase in between is just an intermediate angle.

The eight namesWhat each angle looks like

The named phases are slices of a continuous sweep. Four of them — new, first quarter, full, last quarter — are instants, the moments elongation passes 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°. The other four are the stretches in between, which is why a crescent lasts days and a "full moon" is technically a single second.

The rising times in that table are the part people find surprising. The Moon is not a night-time object that happens to change shape; each phase comes with its own timetable. A waxing crescent is an evening object you will never see at 3 a.m. A waning crescent is a dawn object you will never see in the evening. Half of all moonlight falls in daylight hours, which is why the Moon is so often visible in a blue sky.

Waxing or waning, in one glance

From Britain the rule is a bookkeeping trick: if the lit edge is on the right, the Moon is waxing and is an evening object; if it is on the left, it is waning and belongs to the morning. The lit edge always points at the Sun, so it is really a question of which side of the Sun the Moon has got to. South of the equator the rule flips, because the observer is upside down relative to us.

The local partWhy Exeter changes the picture

The fraction of the disc that is lit is the same from everywhere on Earth. Someone in Sydney sees the identical 37% that someone in Exeter sees, to within a rounding error. What is not the same is the tilt.

The bright limb points at the Sun. To say where that is in a real sky you have to know where the Sun is relative to your own vertical — which depends on your latitude, on the time of year, and on the time of night. Exeter sits at 50.72°N, 3.53°W. From there the crescent that hangs over the Exe after a February sunset is tipped quite differently from the same crescent seen from the tropics, where it lies on its back like a bowl.

The tilt also rotates over the course of a single night. Follow one moon from rise to set in Figure 1 and the horns swing steadily round, because your own "up" is turning with the Earth while the Sun stays put. Nothing about the Moon changed; the observer did.

A detail worth the arithmetic

The Moon is close enough that where you stand matters to its position. Parallax between the centre of the Earth and the surface shifts it by up to a degree — about two Moon-widths, and the difference between "just risen" and "not yet". Figure 1 works in Exeter-topocentric coordinates rather than geocentric ones for that reason.

The calendar problem29.5 days that fit nothing

The Moon completes an orbit in 27.32 days, measured against the stars. But the Earth has moved a twelfth of the way around the Sun in that time, so the Moon has to travel about two days further to get back to the same angle with the Sun. That longer figure — 29.53 days, the synodic month — is the one the phases keep, and the one every lunar calendar is built on.

129.53 = 127.32 − 1365.26
The phases run at the beat frequency between the Moon's orbit and the Earth's. Sidereal month, synodic month, year — only two of the three are independent.

Twelve of those months come to 354.4 days, eleven short of a solar year. That single mismatch is why calendars are hard, and why the answers diverge: the Islamic calendar lets its months drift backwards through the seasons, the Hebrew and Chinese calendars insert a thirteenth month every few years to drag them back, and the Gregorian calendar gives up on the Moon entirely and keeps months only as a name for a length of time. Easter, awkwardly, still needs both.

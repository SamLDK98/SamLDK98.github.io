Completing the Square

Squares and Their Areas

We can think of numbers as squares.

If \(x\) is a side length → the area is \(x^2\).

If \(x\) is the area → \(\sqrt{x}\) is a side length.

For example:

  • 9 → \(3\times3\) square.
  • 6 → side length of square with area \(6^2=36\).

Squaring a Binomial

A binomial is an expression with two terms:

\((a+b)\)

We can also think of \((a+b)\) in terms of squares.

If \((a+b)\) is a side length → the area is \((a+b)^2\).

Looking at the geometry of such a square, we can see there is another way to express the area.

\(a^2+2ab+b^2\)

Therefore, both expressions represent the same value:

\((a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2\)

In other words, we could write any sum of two numbers squared in this expanded form.

Imagine breaking 6 down into a sum of two terms.

\((2+4)^2\)

We could visualise this geometrically to again see why it can also be expressed as:

\((2+4)^2=2^2+2(2\cdot4)+4^2\)

Completing the square will eventually exploit this relationship in reverse, even when the original expression isn't a perfect square trinomial.

Squaring a Binomial with One Variable — Perfect Square Trinomials

If we knew part of a square's side length, we could express the area as, for example:

\((x+3)^2\)

Then, using the identity we previously established:

\((a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2\)

We can substitute \(a=x\) and \(b=3\):

\((x+3)^2=x^2+2(x)(3)+3^2\)

Simplifying:

\(\boxed{(x+3)^2=x^2+6x+9}\)

The expression:

\(x^2+6x+9\)

is called a perfect square trinomial because it is a three-term polynomial that can be expressed as the square of a binomial.

In other words, when we square a binomial with only one variable, we end up with a three-term expression of the form:

\(ax^2+bx+c\)

What does that mean for solving quadratics?

Checking for perfect square trinomials

Evidently, some quadratics can be rewritten as binomials squared, which are easier to solve.

We can check if a quadratic is a perfect square trinomial as follows:

  1. Take the square root of the first term.
  2. Take the square root of the last term.
  3. If the middle term is twice their product, the answer is yes.

For example:

\(x^2+6x+9\)

  1. The first term is \(x^2\), so its square root is \(x\).
  2. The last term is \(9\), so its square root is \(3\).
  3. Check whether the middle term is twice their product: \(2(x)(3)=6x\).

It matches, so \(x^2+6x+9\) can be rewritten as \((a+b)^2\). We can find the values for \(a\) and \(b\) using a factoring method.

Also, if the middle term were \(-6x\), we would use subtraction:

\(x^2-6x+9=(x-3)^2\)

What about non perfect square trinomials?

While squaring a binomial always produces a perfect square trinomial, not every quadratic trinomial is a perfect square.

For example:

\(x^2+6x+5\)

  1. The first term is \(x^2\), so its square root is \(x\).
  2. The last term is \(5\), so its square root is \(\sqrt{5}\).
  3. Check whether the middle term is twice their product: \(2(\sqrt{5}x)\neq6x\).

This quadratic cannot be factored into a perfect square binomial.

Instead, we can ignore the constant and turn \(x^2+6x\) into a square with a corner missing.

Imagine:

  • \(x^2\) as a square \(x\cdot x\).
  • \(6x\) as a rectangle \(6\cdot x\).

We could split the rectangle in two such that we have two thinner rectangles of \(3\cdot x\).

We can then align the \(x\) sides to make a bigger square with a corner missing that has an area of \(3^2\).

Now that we've seen the geometric reasoning we understand why the following procedure works:

Halve the coefficient of \(x\), then square the result.

\(\left(\frac{6}{2}\right)^2=9\)

Adding \(9\) to \(x^2+6x\) creates a perfect square trinomial.

To solve the equation \(x^2+6x+5=0\), we already have a constant \(5\), so we just need to add \(4\) to both sides of the equation.

\(x^2+6x+9=4\)

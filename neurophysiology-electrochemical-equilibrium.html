Electrochemical Equilibrium

The setupA box with two compartments

Imagine a box with two compartments. Both compartments are filled with water.

The wall dividing them is permeable to $K^+$.

Both compartments contain some amount of a potassium chloride solution (KCl), a mixture of $K^+$ and $Cl^-$.

Compartment 1 ($C1$) has a KCl concentration of 1 mM.
Compartment 2 ($C2$) has a KCl concentration of 1 mM.

Initially, there would be no difference in charge between the two compartments. If we measured the electrical charge in each and compared them we would find that the voltage equals 0. This would remain true even over time, despite the wall being selectively permeable to $K^+$, because with equal concentrations on both sides there is nothing driving a net movement: $K^+$ crosses the wall constantly, but at the same rate in each direction, so no charge is ever separated.

If we poured more KCl solution into $C1$, initially there would still be no difference in charge between the two compartments. This is because, despite the concentration being greater in one compartment, the charge of that compartment remains neutral because we have increased $K^+$ and $Cl^-$ proportionally.

However, the difference in concentration between the two compartments creates a gradient which the ions want to cancel. All the ions tend to move towards the area of lower concentration; however, because the wall only permits $K^+$ to pass, there will be a difference in charge created between the two compartments.

That means we are going to have a system where there are two forces pushing in opposing directions:

  • Chemical force (diffusion): The concentration gradient wants more ions to enter $C2$.
  • Electrical force (electrostatics): The charge gradient wants $K^+$ ions to move back into $C1$.

A balance between the two forces, the electrochemical equilibrium, will need to be struck.

For a given concentration difference, we can predict the required electrical potential difference needed to achieve the equilibrium potential using the Nernst equation.

The equationNernst

$$E_{\text{X}} = \frac{RT}{zF} \ln \frac{[\text{X}]_2}{[\text{X}]_1}$$
VariableDefinitionValue
$E_X$ Equilibrium potential for an ion $X$ —
$R$ Universal gas constant $8.314 \frac{\text{J}}{\text{mol} \cdot \text{K}}$
$T$ Absolute temperature (Kelvin) Standard laboratory temp: $293.15 \ \text{K}$ (20 °C)
$z$ Valence (charge) of permeant ion —
$F$ Faraday constant (the amount of electrical potential in 1 mole of a univalent ion) $96{,}485 \frac{\text{C}}{\text{mol}}$
$[X]$ Square brackets around a molecule mean 'concentration of' —

The exchange rate

$\Large \frac{RT}{zF}$  →  this gives us the 'exchange rate': for every unit of kinetic energy acting on ions, how much electrical potential we need to cancel it out. It converts joules to volts.

$RT$  →  this quantifies the kinetic energy available to move ions at a given temperature.
$zF$  →  this represents the amount of electrical charge in one mole of ions.

$$RT = \left(8.314 \, \frac{\text{J}}{\text{mol} \cdot \text{K}}\right) \times 293.15 \text{ K} \approx 2437 \text{ J/mol}$$

Dividing this by $F$ converts that molar kinetic energy into an electrical potential in volts (or millivolts).

$$\frac{2437 \frac{\text{J}}{\text{mol}}}{96{,}485 \frac{\text{C}}{\text{mol}}}$$ $$\frac{2437\text{ J}}{\text{mol}} \cdot \frac{1\text{ mol}}{96{,}485\text{ C}}$$ $$\frac{2437\text{ J}}{96{,}485\text{ C}}$$ $$\frac{2437}{96{,}485} \cdot \frac{\text{J}}{\text{C}}$$ $$0.02526 \frac{\text{J}}{\text{C}}$$

Since a volt ($V$) $= \frac{\text{J}}{\text{C}}$:

$$0.02526\text{ V}$$

To convert V to mV, multiply by 1,000:

$$25.26\text{ mV}$$

If one side has a concentration $e$ times greater than the other, there would need to be $25.26\text{ mV}$ of a difference in charge between the sides to cancel it out.

The size of the gradient

Now we have:

$$E_{\text{K}} = 25.26 \ln \frac{[\text{K}]_2}{[\text{K}]_1}$$

$\Large \frac{[\text{K}]_2}{[\text{K}]_1}$  →  this term tells us how big the concentration gradient is.

$$\frac{1\text{ mM}}{10\text{ mM}} = \frac{1}{10} = 0.1$$

For every 1 unit of potassium on side 2, we have 10 units on side 1.

We now know that the concentration difference is 10-fold (0.1). However, this is in terms of the actual physical count of ions.

If the energy required to push back against concentration gradients scaled linearly, we could simply use this ratio. However, it doesn't. It scales logarithmically. So:

$$\ln(0.1) \approx -2.3026$$

This $-2.3026$ is our exact multiplier. It tells us that our 10-to-1 gradient is exactly 2.3026 times stronger than a standard $e$-to-1 gradient (and the negative sign just indicates the direction of the flow).

To find the final voltage, we multiply our baseline exchange rate ($25.26\text{ mV}$, the voltage needed for exactly one $e$-fold gradient) by this new multiplier:

$$E_{\text{K}} = 25.26\ \text{mV} \ \cdot \ -2.3026$$ $$E_{\text{K}} = -58.16 \ \text{mV}$$

Therefore, we need $-58.16\text{ mV}$ of electrical potential to perfectly cancel out the chemical push of a 10-to-1 potassium gradient.

Reading the signNegative relative to what?

The minus sign is doing real work, and it is worth being explicit about what it refers to. With $[\text{X}]_2$ on top and $[\text{X}]_1$ underneath, the answer comes out as the potential of $C1$ relative to $C2$.

That matches the physical picture. $C1$ is the concentrated side, so $K^+$ leaves it; every ion that goes takes a positive charge with it and leaves its $Cl^-$ partner behind. $C1$ therefore becomes negative with respect to $C2$ — hence $-58.16\text{ mV}$. In the figure above, the minus signs collect on the $C1$ face of the wall.

Why this convention is the useful one

Map $C1$ onto the inside of a cell and $C2$ onto the outside, and this is exactly the convention used for neurons. Run a real cell's numbers through the figure — 140 mM inside, 4 mM outside, at 37 °C — and it returns $-95.4\text{ mV}$, which is where the resting potassium equilibrium of about $-90\text{ mV}$ comes from.

Worth tryingThree things the figure will show you

  • Equal concentrations give exactly zero. Set both compartments to the same value: $\ln(1) = 0$, so $E = 0$ no matter how concentrated they are. Concentration alone creates no voltage — only a difference does.
  • The line is straight on a log axis. Every tenfold change adds the same $58.2\text{ mV}$ at 20 °C. A hundredfold gradient is not ten times the voltage of a tenfold one, it is twice it.
  • Valence divides. Switch the permeant ion to $Ca^{2+}$ and the same gradient gives half the voltage, because $z$ sits underneath. Switch to $Cl^-$ and the sign flips, because $z$ is negative.

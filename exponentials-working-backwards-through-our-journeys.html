Exponentials

Working Backwards Through Our Journeys

Let's consider two journeys along the number line: one additive and one multiplicative (without reference to coefficients or exponents).

We just want to reach some target value:

\[256\]

For an additive journey, we start at \(0\), the additive identity.

For a multiplicative journey, we start at \(1\), the multiplicative identity.

A Journey in Additive Steps

Let's say we want to take steps of equal distance.

  • If our steps cover a distance of \(1\), it will take \(256\) steps.
  • If our steps cover a distance of \(2\), it will take \(128\) steps.
  • If our steps cover a distance of \(4\), it will take \(64\) steps.

A Journey in Multiplicative Steps

Let's say we take steps of equal ratios.

The next step must always be \(x\) times as big (or small) as the previous number.

We start at \(1\).

If our steps must have a ratio of \(2\), it will take \(8\) steps.

\[1 \to 2 \to 4 \to 8 \to 16 \to 32 \to 64 \to 128 \to 256\]

If our steps must have a ratio of \(4\), it will take \(4\) steps.

\[1 \to 4 \to 16 \to 64 \to 256\]

If our steps must have a ratio of \(16\), it will take \(2\) steps.

\[1 \to 16 \to 256\]

Working Backwards

So far, we have considered how addition and multiplication allow us to move towards a target value in equal steps.

But what if we already know our target?

This is where inverse operations come into play.

Going Backwards from an Additive Journey

If we assume an additive journey to reach the target value, we can work out:

  • The distance each step must travel.
  • The number of steps that must be taken.

We can work this out using division.

If we know the step size:

\[\frac{256}{32}=8 \qquad \qquad \frac{\text{output}}{\text{step size}}= \text{number of steps}\]

If we know the number of steps:

\[\frac{256}{8}=32 \qquad \qquad \frac{\text{output}}{\text{number of steps}}= \text{step size}\]

Notice that we can use division to recover either of these values if we know the value of the other.

Examples:

\[\begin{array}{c|c|c} \text{Target} & \text{Step Size} & \text{Number of Steps} \\ \hline 256 & +1 & \frac{256}{1}=256 \\ 256 & +2 & \frac{256}{2}=128 \\ 256 & +4 & \frac{256}{4}=64 \end{array}\]
\[\begin{array}{c|c|c} \text{Target} & \text{Number of Steps} & \text{Step Size} \\ \hline 256 & 2 & \frac{256}{2}=128 \\ 256 & 4 & \frac{256}{4}=64 \\ 256 & 8 & \frac{256}{8}=32 \end{array}\]

Going Backwards from a Multiplicative Journey

If we assume a multiplicative journey, we require a different operation to recover each possible value: the step ratio and the number of steps taken.

If we know the step ratio, logarithms can identify the number of multiplicative steps needed to reach an output:

\[\log_{2}(256)=8 \qquad \qquad \log_{\text{ratio}} (\text{output})= \text{number of steps}\]

If we know the number of multiplicative steps we need to take, but not the multiplicative factor, we use radication (taking roots).

The radicand tells us the target output we must reach. The index tells us how many multiplicative steps we must take. The result, known as the root, tells us what multiplicative factor we need at each step.

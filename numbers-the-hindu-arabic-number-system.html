The Hindu-Arabic Number System

Ten symbols, and a rule that the place a symbol sits in changes what it is worth. It is so familiar that it is easy to miss how impressive a technology it is.

IntroductionOne quantity, five digits

36,952
thirty-six thousand, nine hundred and fifty-two

This is a single quantity. It is represented here by five digits, in a particular order. That representation is a numeral of the Hindu-Arabic number system.

Three words are worth keeping apart:

  • Digits are the elementary symbols: 0, 1, 2 … 9.
  • Numerals are written representations of numbers, built from digits: 36,952.
  • Numbers are the concept itself — the quantity the numeral points at.

Our familiarity with this system hides how impressive a technology it is. To see how clever it is, it helps to consider how numbers came to be.

Before symbolsCounting by one-to-one correspondence

We have no detailed account of how the ability to count developed, but it is generally believed that physical representations of numbers came before symbolic ones. Without some physical apparatus, early humans may only have had a sense of one, two, three, and many.

Tallying might have meant cutting notches into bone, or tying knots in string, to stand for the size of some other group of things.

Or imagine a shepherd who puts a pebble in a bag for each sheep he lets out to graze. When the flock comes back, he takes one pebble out for each sheep that returns. If any pebbles are left over, he knows he is missing that many sheep.

This kind of counting physically carries out a one-to-one correspondence — one pebble for one sheep — but it does not require the counter to have any mental representation of numbers while doing it. The shepherd never needs to know how many sheep he has. The Origins of Counting follows this stage in more detail.

WordsOral counting

Humans probably began with words for quantities of specific things. There may have been one word for three sheep and another for three apples, but no word for the abstract idea of three. Traces of this survive in languages that still count different kinds of objects with different number words.

At some stage in prehistory, though, we must have developed spoken symbols for quantities in the abstract — number words generalised beyond any particular thing being counted.

MarksWritten counting

Numbers were among the very first things ever written down. The oldest Mesopotamian tablets are mostly accounts, and writing itself seems to have grown out of keeping them.

We call the early systems sign-value systems: a sign denotes an abstract quantity, its value.

The simplest sign-value system has one symbol for 1 — say a line — and writes every other number as that many lines. This is only a slight extension of the physical one-to-one mapping that preceded it, and the reader still has to count the lines, in their head, using the words they have for numbers.

As a thought experiment, imagine the opposite extreme: a system in which every quantity has its own unique symbol. Then any symbol would tell you its quantity at a glance — if you had memorised which quantity goes with which symbol. That might be feasible up to thirty or so, but memorising unique symbols clearly has a low ceiling. Neither extreme scales.

The first breakthroughGrouping quantities

The first big breakthrough was to give unique symbols to only some quantities, and to build the rest by addition.

Across the ancient Near East, from around 8,000 BCE, people kept accounts with small clay tokens of different shapes. By the late fourth millennium BCE, Sumerian scribes were pressing number signs into wet clay tablets, and those signs echo the token shapes: a small cone-shaped mark for 1, a small circle for 10, and a large cone-shaped mark for 60.

So to write a number like 86, you only need to have memorised three symbols, and instead of counting 86 marks you count nine: one 60, two 10s and six 1s.

86 = 60 + 10 + 10 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1
the addition needed to read the number was likely done on a physical apparatus — counters, or fingers

We call these systems additive, because under the hood the reader is computing a + b + c + d + …, where each term has a fixed value. The symbols that stand for those fixed values are the base symbols: the building blocks of the system.

Some examples of early systems like this, each writing the number 352:

Egyptian hieroglyphs

The ancient Egyptians (from around 3,000 BCE) used a base-10 system: every base symbol is a power of ten — a stroke for 1, a heel bone for 10, a coiled rope for 100, a lotus for 1,000. We see ten appear a lot across cultures, possibly because we have ten fingers, which could have served in the one-to-one counting that came first.

Roman numerals

The Romans used letters for their base symbols, mixing fives and tens:

I = 1, V = 5, X = 10, L = 50, C = 100, D = 500, M = 1000

The values are added, except that a smaller numeral written just before a larger one is subtracted from it, so IV is 4 and XC is 90. (The Romans themselves were inconsistent about this — IIII for 4 is common on Roman inscriptions — and the subtractive rule was standardised later.)

Greek Attic numerals

The Greeks of Athens used the initial letter of the number word as its symbol: Π (pente) for 5, Δ (deka) for 10, H (hekaton) for 100, X (khilioi) for 1,000. The exception is 1, which is a plain stroke, I. They also combined signs: a Δ written inside a Π means five tens, 50.

The second breakthroughPositional notation

These additive systems have a limitation. Each symbol has a fixed value: the symbol for 10 means 10 wherever you put it. And because the values are simply added up, and addition does not care about order, the arrangement of the symbols carries no information of its own. HHΔ and ΔHH mean the same.

So to represent larger and larger quantities, you have two choices:

  1. Keep inventing new symbols.
  2. Keep repeating the symbols you already have, giving longer and messier numerals.

The beauty of the Hindu-Arabic system is that it reuses the same small set of symbols indefinitely. It does this by giving each symbol's value another dimension: its position.

We have a unique symbol for each of the numbers 1 through 9. To represent the next value, ten, instead of creating a new symbol we start counting groups of ten, and open a new column to hold that count:

  • 10 — the 1 denotes one group of ten.

But the 1 needs something to its right, or it would just read as one. That is the job of the tenth symbol, 0: it marks a column that is empty and holds the other digits in their places. Without a zero, positional notation does not work.

Counting on to 19, to move to the next value we simply count two groups of ten:

  • 20 — the 2 denotes two groups of ten.

After 99, we repeat the procedure, and create a new position to count groups of one hundred:

  • 100 — a 1 in the third place indicates one group of one hundred.
  • 111 — each 1 counts a different size of group: one hundred, one ten, and one one.

Take the number 352:

  • 2 — two 1s.
  • 5 — five 10s.
  • 3 — three 100s.

In our decimal system, every position stands for a different power of ten:

352 = 3(100) + 5(10) + 2(1)
352 = 3(102) + 5(101) + 2(100)
the second line is the more revealing: the position of a digit is the power of ten it multiplies

This is an enormous compression of information. Ten symbols are enough for any number at all, and a number a thousand times larger needs only three more digits. Egyptian needs ten signs to write 352 and Roman six; we need three.

The idea of position was not new — Babylonian scribes had used it, in base 60, two thousand years earlier, without a true zero. What India added, by around the seventh century CE, was a base of ten with zero as a digit and a number in its own right. The system reached the Islamic world, where al-Khwārizmī wrote on calculating with Hindu numerals, and reached Europe largely through Fibonacci in the thirteenth century — which is why it carries both names. The History of Numbers tells that story.

What the notation buysArithmetic becomes an algorithm

Compression is only half of it. The bigger payoff is that positional notation turns arithmetic into a procedure — a fixed set of small steps that works the same way on numbers of any size.

Because every column means a power of ten, two numbers can be lined up by their right-hand ends, and then each column can be dealt with on its own. Adding two columns only ever involves two single digits, so you only ever need one hundred facts — 0 + 0 up to 9 + 9 — and one rule for when a column overflows: carry one ten into the next column left. Multiplication works the same way with the times table, plus one gift from the notation: multiplying by ten is just shifting every digit one place left and writing a 0.

Try a six-digit sum, then a two-digit one. The numbers change; the table does not. Every step anywhere in the working is one cell of it, plus a carry. Arrow keys step once you have clicked inside the figure.

It is not that additive systems had no arithmetic. Adding Roman or Egyptian numerals is straightforward enough: pool the symbols, and swap every ten strokes for a heel bone, every five I's for a V. The Egyptians multiplied by repeated doubling, and Roman merchants calculated on a counting board and only wrote down the result.

What positional notation adds is a method that is uniform, local and bounded. Uniform, because every column is handled the same way. Local, because each step looks at only a couple of digits. Bounded, because the facts you need fit in two ten-by-ten tables, however large the numbers get. Those three properties are what let a child learn long multiplication, and what let the same procedure be written into a machine — the word algorithm itself comes from al-Khwārizmī's name.

The test of a good notation

A good notation does some of your thinking for you. In 36,952 the structure of the number — three ten-thousands, six thousands, nine hundreds, five tens, two ones — is laid out where the eye and the pencil can work on it directly, one column at a time.

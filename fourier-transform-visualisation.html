From a complex signal, we can determine which sinusoidal waves are within it: their frequencies, amplitudes and phases.

Imagine a wave at 3 Hz, an amplitude of 1, and phase of 0 (we’ll call it the test wave):

If we multiply the displacements at each point of the test and complex waves, we create a product wave:

We take the average displacement of the points on the product wave: we add up all the displacements, keeping their signs, and divide by how many there are.

If the test frequency is not in the complex signal, the average is always 0, as long as we average over whole cycles:

But why?

Sinusoidal waves have as much displacement above the centre line as below. Each half of an oscillation is a mirror image of the other, so over whole cycles the + and − cancel and the average is always 0.

Whenever we multiply by a negative, the sign of the other number is flipped. So whenever the test wave is negative, it flips the original wave. At every point, it also scales the original wave by the test wave’s size there.

If the test wave is a different frequency to the original wave, it flips as much of its tops as its bottoms. This preserves the average as 0.

The exception is when the frequency is the same: the negatives always line up, so every bottom is flipped up and everything becomes positive.

Multiplying a complex wave by a test wave is the same as multiplying each of its constituent waves and adding them.

This is the distributive property. Just as 3 × 10 = 3 × 2 + 3 × 8, multiplying the complex wave by the test wave gives the same result as multiplying each of its waves by the test wave and adding them up. The average works the same way: the average of the product wave is the averages of those separate products added together.

We have already seen what each of those products does. Any wave at a different frequency from the test wave averages to 0. So if the test frequency is absent, every part contributes 0, and the total is 0. If it is present, that one part leaves a value behind, and that value reflects its amplitude.

How big is it?

Look again at the same-frequency figure. The 2 Hz wave there has an amplitude of 3 µV, and the dashed average line sits at 1.5 µV, exactly half. This is always the case: with a test wave of amplitude 1, a matching wave of amplitude A that is lined up with it leaves an average of A/2. So doubling the average recovers the amplitude.

Waves whose product averages to zero are called orthogonal. The same property is what makes power add up across frequencies, explained in Why Power Adds Up.

What is its phase?

So far, the 2 Hz wave and the test wave have both started at phase 0, so their peaks and troughs line up. But a wave in a complex signal can be at any point in its cycle. Shift the 2 Hz wave by a quarter of a cycle:

Now the test wave’s below-the-line stretches catch the 2 Hz wave’s tops and bottoms equally, just as with different frequencies, and the average is 0. The frequency is present, but the test misses it. At other phases the test only partly catches it: depending on the phase, the average can be anywhere from half the amplitude down to minus half.

The fix is to use two test waves at each frequency, a quarter of a cycle apart: a sine and a cosine. Whatever the wave’s phase, it lines up partly with one and partly with the other:

Change the wave’s phase. As one average shrinks, the other grows. Treat the two averages as the two sides of a right-angled triangle: the long side is always half the amplitude, 1.5 µV, whatever the phase, so doubling it gives back 3 µV. The angle of the triangle is the wave’s phase. Mathematically, the pair of averages is usually packaged as a single complex number, using Euler’s formula.

Testing every frequency

Here is a more realistic complex wave. It contains a wave at every frequency from 1 to 15 Hz, each with its own amplitude and phase, but you can’t see them by eye. Slide through the test frequencies, or press Test all. At each frequency, the sine and cosine test waves recover that wave’s amplitude, and a bar appears. Once every frequency has been tested, the hidden recipe is shown for comparison.

This is the Fourier transform: the same test at every frequency. The bars are the complex wave’s amplitude spectrum.

Switch to Sine only to see why the second test wave matters. Without it, each bar depends on its wave’s phase as well as its amplitude, and some even come out negative.

Now switch to Power. Each bar becomes half its amplitude squared, and together they form a power spectrum. Their total matches the complex wave’s own average power exactly. Amplitudes don’t do this. We never needed power to find the waves; we need it when we want the parts to add up to the whole. Why power has this property is the subject of Why Power Adds Up. On a real recording, the same ideas underlie PSD estimation.

How to Solve It

The trick is to treat the signal as two separate waves and deal with the bigger, slower one first. The faster one is the small ripple riding on top of it.

1. Find the slow wave’s frequency

Ignore the ripple and follow the big swell. Count how many grid squares one full cycle of it takes, from one crest to the next.

ω = 4 ÷ (grid squares per cycle)
  • 8 squares (one cycle across the whole board): ω = 0.5
  • 4 squares: ω = 1
  • 2 squares: ω = 2

2. Estimate its amplitude

Imagine a line running through the middle of the ripple. The amplitude is how far that line rises above the dotted centre line at its highest. The faint horizontal lines mark heights of 0.5 and 1, so you can read it off the board.

3. Read its phase from the left edge

Look at where the slow swell starts on the left edge of the board, and which way it is heading:

PhaseThe wave starts…
0on the centre line, heading up
0.5πat its highest point
πon the centre line, heading down
1.5πat its lowest point

Positions in between have phases in between.

4. Set the drop to its mirror image

Use the same frequency and amplitude, and add 1π (half a turn) to the phase. On the board, your wave should start at the opposite height to the swell and head the opposite way: wherever the swell is up, your wave is down.

5. Drop, then repeat with the ripple

If the first drop was right, the slow swell vanishes and the leftover is a clean, even ripple: a single wave. Read its frequency, amplitude and phase in the same way, and drop its mirror image.

If the leftover still swells

  • A swell in step with the original means your amplitude was too small; one upside down means it was too big.
  • A swell shifted sideways means the phase was off.
  • A new wiggle at a different speed means the frequency was wrong: undo and recount the grid squares.

“Undo drop” takes back a drop, and “show hint” draws the mirror image of the biggest wave left, so you can check your settings against it.

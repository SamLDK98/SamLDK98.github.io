Sinusoidal Tetris

Adding Waves

In the game, every wave that lands is added to the signal. So before flattening anything, we need to know what adding two waves actually does.

Adding Point by Point

Adding two waves means adding their heights at every moment.

Slide along the board. At each point, the amber height and the blue height are stacked, and the white curve is where the stack ends.

Where both waves are up, the sum is higher than either. Where one is up and the other is down, they partly cancel. A height below the centre line counts as negative, so it pulls the sum down.

Same Frequency: Still One Wave

When the two waves have the same frequency, something tidy happens: their sum is always a single wave, at that same frequency.

Only its size changes, and that depends on how far apart the two phases are. Turn the blue wave’s phase:

  • In step (phases equal): the crests line up and the sizes add. \(1 + 1 = 2\).
  • A quarter turn apart: the sum is in between, about \(1.41\).
  • Half a turn apart: every crest meets a trough, and the sum is flat. \(1 - 1 = 0\).

The circles on the left show why. Each wave is an arm going round a circle, and adding the waves adds the arms tip to tail. The white arm is the sum: it is just another arm going round at the same speed, so the sum is another wave. When the two arms point in opposite directions, the white arm shrinks to nothing.

This is why a drop that is nearly right leaves a small wave behind, rather than a mess: the leftover is still one wave at the same frequency, only smaller.

Different Frequencies: a New Shape

Waves at different frequencies do not merge into one wave. One arm goes round faster than the other, so they drift in and out of step, and the sum takes on a more complicated shape.

Change the blue wave’s frequency. Only when it matches the amber wave does the sum become a single wave again.

This is the signal in Cancel Two Waves: two waves at different frequencies, added together.

Taking a Shape Apart

The game runs this in reverse. It hands you a complicated shape and asks you to find the simple waves inside it, then cancel them one at a time.

A wave at one frequency can only be cancelled by a wave at the same frequency. A drop at any other frequency just adds a new wave to the pile.

Splitting a complicated signal into simple waves is the idea behind the Fourier transform, which is how scientists find the rhythms inside signals such as brain recordings.

Sinusoidal Tetris

Adding Waves

How Phase Affects the Sum

Adding two waves means adding their heights at every point in time.

If two sinusoidal waves have the same frequency and are in phase (they have the same phase), their amplitudes add together.

If they have the same frequency and amplitude but are half a cycle out of phase, every crest of one wave coincides with a trough of the other.

Their heights cancel at every point, producing a flat line.

Phase may increase from \(0\) to \(2\pi\) radians.

Watch what happens to the summed wave as the phase of one wave changes.

As the phase approaches a half turn, the amplitude of the summed wave decreases.

When it reaches the half turn, they cancel completely.

As the phase begins moving from the half turn back towards \(0\), the amplitude of the summed wave begins increasing again.

In Terms of Rotating Radii

Each wave is produced by a point going around a circle.

We can forget the circumference of the circle and think just about rotating radii.

Adding two waves corresponds to adding these rotating radii as vectors, placing the beginning of one at the end of the other.

The vertical position of the resulting vector gives the sum of the two waves.

Notice how the length of the resulting vector depends on the difference between the phases of the original waves.

  • When the vectors point in the same direction, their lengths add.
  • When they point in opposite directions, their lengths subtract.
  • When they have equal lengths and point in opposite directions, they cancel completely.

Different Frequencies: a New Shape

Waves at different frequencies do not merge into one wave. One arm goes round faster than the other, so they drift in and out of step, and the sum takes on a more complicated shape.

Change the blue wave’s frequency. Only when it matches the amber wave does the sum become a single wave again.

This is the signal in Cancel Two Waves: two waves at different frequencies, added together.

Taking a Shape Apart

The game runs this in reverse. It hands you a complicated shape and asks you to find the simple waves inside it, then cancel them one at a time.

A wave at one frequency can only be cancelled by a wave at the same frequency. A drop at any other frequency just adds a new wave to the pile.

Splitting a complicated signal into simple waves is the idea behind the Fourier transform, which is how scientists find the rhythms inside signals such as brain recordings.

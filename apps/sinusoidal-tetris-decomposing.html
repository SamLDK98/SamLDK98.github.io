Sinusoidal Tetris

Decomposing a Composite Signal

Here is a wave made by adding two sinusoidal waves with different frequencies: a slow one and a fast one.

When we add a faster wave to a slower wave, we can think of the slower wave as becoming the centre line of the faster wave.

Because the faster wave oscillates equally above and below this line, the combined wave appears to ripple around the slower wave.

If we imagine tracing a line through the middle of these ripples, we can visualise the underlying slow wave.

When the slow wave’s amplitude is much greater than the fast wave’s, its contribution to the height of the composite wave is large enough to make it visually clear.

However, as the fast wave’s amplitude increases, the ripples get bigger.

If the slow wave has only a small amplitude, the centre line of the composite wave may only sway up and down ever so slightly, as the slow wave is not contributing much to the height at each point.

Spotting the Slow Wave

The ripples ride highest where the slow wave has a crest, and sink lowest where it has a trough.

So the quickest way to find the slow wave is to look for those places first, then read its frequency and phase from them.

Try to create the slow wave that features in the following composite waves by adjusting the frequency and phase. In these first examples, the slow wave’s amplitude is much greater than the fast wave’s, so it is visually clear.

Now try some where the ripples are bigger.

Reading Each Wave

Once a wave has been separated out, read its three properties off the board.

Frequency. The board is split into 8 columns. Count how many columns one full cycle takes, from one crest to the next:

  • 8 columns (one cycle across the whole board): \(\omega = 0.5\)
  • 4 columns: \(\omega = 1\)
  • 2 columns: \(\omega = 2\)
  • 1 column: \(\omega = 4\)

In general, \(\omega = 4 \div\) (columns per cycle). For the fast wave it is often easier to count its crests across the whole board and halve the count.

Amplitude. For the slow wave, it is the height of the middle line’s crests above the centre line. For the fast wave, it is half the thickness of the ripple.

Phase. Look at where each wave starts at the left edge, and which way it is heading:

PhaseThe wave starts…
0on its centre line, heading up
0.5πat a crest
πon its centre line, heading down
1.5πat a trough

For the fast wave, “its centre line” is the slow wave: look at where the ripple starts relative to it.

Try It

Set the dials for the slow and fast waves. Your two waves added together are drawn as a dashed line. When it lies on top of the white wave, you have decomposed it.

When It Gets Harder

  • Similar amplitudes. If the fast wave is as big as the slow one, there is no clear “ripple”. The middle-line trick still works, but the middle is harder to see.
  • Similar frequencies. If the two frequencies are close, the waves drift slowly in and out of step and the sum swells and fades in beats. The slow wave is no longer obvious at all.

In the game, you do not need to find both waves at once. Cancel the one you are surest of; whatever is left becomes easier to read.

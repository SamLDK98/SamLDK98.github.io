Polynomials

The Quadratic Formula

On Polynomials, we built the class of quadratic equations and said that creating a formula for them is not as straightforward as it was for linear equations.

Here it is. For any quadratic equation

\[ax^2+bx+c=0\qquad(a\neq0)\]

the solutions are

\[\boxed{x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}}\]

Test it on an equation we already know. \(x^2-5x+6=0\) has solutions \(2\) and \(3\). Here \(a=1\), \(b=-5\), \(c=6\):

\[x=\frac{5\pm\sqrt{25-24}}{2}=\frac{5\pm1}{2}\]

So \(x=3\) or \(x=2\).

Like \(x=\frac{c-b}{a}\) for linear equations, it is a general solution formula. It expresses \(x\) in terms of the known quantities \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\), so it solves every quadratic at once.

But why does it look like this?

Reading the Formula

Split it into two fractions:

\[x=-\frac{b}{2a}\pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\]

The first piece, \(-\frac{b}{2a}\), is where the graph turns. The line \(x=-\frac{b}{2a}\) is its axis of symmetry.

The solutions are the two inputs whose output is \(0\). Inputs with equal outputs sit either side of the axis, equally far from it.

The second piece is the step from the axis to a solution. The \(\pm\) takes it both ways: one way gives one solution, the other way gives the other.

So the formula says: go to the axis, then step the same amount either way.

For \(x^2-5x+6=0\), the axis is at \(2.5\) and the step is \(0.5\).

Why We Can't Just Isolate \(x\)

A linear equation is solved by undoing what was done to \(x\), in reverse. Try that here:

\[ax^2+bx=-c\]

\(x\) appears twice: once squared, once not. A square root would undo \(x^2\), but not while \(bx\) is attached. \(\sqrt{ax^2+bx}\) is not anything simpler.

So we need another way in. There are two: through the pair of solutions, and through the symmetry of the graph.

Building It from the Pair

On Multiple Solutions (2), we solved \(x^2-5x+6=0\) by finding two numbers with sum \(5\) and product \(6\). We wrote them as \(2.5-d\) and \(2.5+d\) and found \(d\).

The quadratic formula is the same calculation, with letters.

First divide through by \(a\), so the \(x^2\) term has coefficient \(1\). We can, because \(a\neq0\), and it doesn't change the solutions: \(0\div a\) is still \(0\).

\[x^2+\frac{b}{a}x+\frac{c}{a}=0\]

From Expansion, the solutions of a monic quadratic add to the negative of its linear coefficient and multiply to its constant term:

\[\text{sum}=-\frac{b}{a}\qquad\text{product}=\frac{c}{a}\]

Two numbers with sum \(-\frac{b}{a}\) have average \(-\frac{b}{2a}\). Call it \(m\). The numbers are \(m-d\) and \(m+d\) for some \(d\).

Their product is a difference of two squares:

\[(m-d)(m+d)=m^2-d^2\]

It must equal \(\frac{c}{a}\):

\[d^2=m^2-\frac{c}{a}=\frac{b^2}{4a^2}-\frac{c}{a}\]

To combine the fractions, write \(\frac{c}{a}\) over \(4a^2\): it becomes \(\frac{4ac}{4a^2}\).

\[d^2=\frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}\]

That's where \(4ac\) comes from.

If \(b^2-4ac\) is negative, so is \(d^2\). No real number squares to a negative, so there is no pair. We'll come back to that. Otherwise, take the square root:

\[d=\pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\]

(Strictly, \(\sqrt{4a^2}\) is \(2a\) or \(-2a\), depending on the sign of \(a\). With \(\pm\) in front, it makes no difference.)

The solutions are \(m\pm d\):

\[\begin{aligned}x&=-\frac{b}{2a}\pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\\ &=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\end{aligned}\]

Every symbol now has a source:

  • \(-b\): multiplying out \((x-r)(x-s)\) gives \(x^2-(r+s)x+rs\), so the sum of the solutions arrives with a minus sign. Half of it is the centre.
  • \(2a\): the \(2\) from averaging two numbers, the \(a\) from dividing through. The step has it too, from \(\sqrt{4a^2}\).
  • \(b^2-4ac\): the centre squared, minus the product, scaled up by \(4a^2\) to clear the fractions.
  • \(\sqrt{\ }\) and \(\pm\): \(d\) was squared in the product; the root undoes it, in both directions.

Building It by Completing the Square

On Axis of Symmetry, we measured inputs from the turning point, and \(x^2+bx\) became \(k^2-\frac{b^2}{4}\): a single square, and a constant. The same move works on any quadratic.

Start again from the monic form. Squaring \(x+\frac{b}{2a}\) gives the same first two terms, \(x^2+\frac{b}{a}x\), plus an extra \(\frac{b^2}{4a^2}\):

\[\left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2=x^2+\frac{b}{a}x+\frac{b^2}{4a^2}\]

So swap those two terms for the square, minus that extra:

\[\begin{aligned}x^2+\frac{b}{a}x+\frac{c}{a}&=0\\ \left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2-\frac{b^2}{4a^2}+\frac{c}{a}&=0\\ \left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2&=\frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}\end{aligned}\]

Now \(x\) appears only once, inside a square, so a square root can undo it:

\[\begin{aligned}x+\frac{b}{2a}&=\pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\\ x&=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\end{aligned}\]

This is called completing the square.

Look at the line before the square root. It is \(d^2=\frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}\) again, with \(x+\frac{b}{2a}\), the step from the axis, in place of \(d\). The two roads meet.

The Discriminant

The expression under the square root, \(b^2-4ac\), is called the discriminant, often written \(\Delta\). It decides how many solutions there are.

Completing the square turned the monic form into a single square and a constant. With \(m=-\frac{b}{2a}\), so that \(x+\frac{b}{2a}\) is \(x-m\):

\[y=(x-m)^2-\frac{\Delta}{4a^2}\]

That is the graph of \(x^2\), moved so its lowest point is at \(x=m\), at a depth of \(\frac{\Delta}{4a^2}\) below the axis.

Step \(t\) away from \(m\) and the curve rises by \(t^2\). To climb back to the axis, it needs \(t^2=\frac{\Delta}{4a^2}\), so \(t=\pm\frac{\sqrt\Delta}{2a}\): the formula's second piece. The step is the square root of the depth.

  • \(\Delta>0\): the lowest point is below the axis. Two solutions.
  • \(\Delta=0\): the lowest point sits on the axis. One solution, \(m\): a repeated root, of multiplicity two.
  • \(\Delta<0\): the lowest point is above the axis, and the curve never reaches it. No real solutions. (The complex numbers are where equations like this find theirs.)

The last case is the \(6.25\) ceiling from Multiple Solutions (2), in letters: \(\Delta<0\) exactly when the product \(\frac{c}{a}\) is more than \(m^2\).

Using the Formula

  1. Rearrange to \(ax^2+bx+c=0\). (If the equation is \(ax^2+bx+c=d\), subtract \(d\) first.)
  2. Read off \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\), with their signs.
  3. Work out \(\Delta=b^2-4ac\). If it is negative, stop: there are no real solutions.
  4. Substitute into \(x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}\), putting each number in brackets.
  5. Check: the solutions should add to \(-\frac{b}{a}\) and multiply to \(\frac{c}{a}\).

Imagine:

\[2x^2=3x+5\]

Rearrange: \(2x^2-3x-5=0\), so \(a=2\), \(b=-3\), \(c=-5\).

\[\begin{aligned}\Delta&=(-3)^2-4(2)(-5)\\ &=9+40=49\end{aligned}\]
\[x=\frac{3\pm7}{4}\]
\[x=2.5\quad\text{or}\quad x=-1\]

Check: \(2.5+(-1)=1.5=\frac{3}{2}\), and \(2.5\times(-1)=-2.5=-\frac{5}{2}\).

When factorising fails, the formula doesn't:

\[x^2-2x-1=0\]

No pair of integers adds to \(2\) and multiplies to \(-1\). Here \(a=1\), \(b=-2\), \(c=-1\), so \(\Delta=4+4=8\).

The centre is \(-\frac{b}{2a}=1\). The step is \(\frac{\sqrt8}{2}=\sqrt{\frac{8}{4}}=\sqrt2\).

\[x=1\pm\sqrt2\]

When \(\Delta=0\): \(x^2+6x+9=0\) has \(\Delta=36-36=0\), so the step is \(0\) and \(x=-3\).

When \(\Delta<0\): \(x^2-5x+7=0\) has \(\Delta=25-28=-3\). No real solutions.

When a quadratic does factorise over the integers, factorising is usually quicker. The formula is for when it doesn't, or when you can't tell.

Common slips:

  • Not rearranging to \(=0\) first. In \(2x^2=3x+5\), \(c\) is \(-5\), not \(5\).
  • When \(b\) is negative, \(-b\) is positive, and \(b^2\) is still positive: \((-3)^2=9\).
  • When \(a\) and \(c\) have opposite signs, \(-4ac\) is positive: \(-4(2)(-5)=+40\).
  • \(2a\) divides the whole numerator, not just the square root.

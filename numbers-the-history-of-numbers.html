The History of Numbers

The ten digits you were taught at school are a technology, and a late one. Getting to them meant solving three separate problems — and the last of the three is the reason you can multiply on paper at all.

Where this startsCounting came first

People kept exact counts for tens of thousands of years before anyone wrote a number. A notch for each hide, a pebble for each sheep: that is counting, and the previous chapter follows it from the first cut bones to the clay tokens of the Fertile Crescent.

This chapter is about the next problem. Once you can count, how do you write it down — in a way that stays readable when the numbers get big, and that lets you calculate rather than merely record?

Problem oneA long record is unreadable

A tally is perfect and useless. Perfect, because one mark per thing cannot be wrong. Useless, because nobody can look at |||||||||||||||||||||||||| and say how many that is without counting it again — and two people counting it will disagree.

The first fix is grouping: crossing off every fifth mark, or cutting in blocks of five, so the eye can read the total in chunks. That alone is already an enormous gain, and it is visible on bones tens of thousands of years old. But it only postpones the problem. Six hundred sheep is still six hundred marks.

Problem twoA new sign for every size

The second fix is to invent a sign that means a group. Egypt did it with a stroke for 1, a heel bone for 10, a coiled rope for 100 and a lotus for 1,000. Rome did it with I, V, X, L, C, D and M. Write the signs, add up what they are worth, and the number is however much they come to. These are additive systems, and they are a vast improvement: 1,999 takes 28 marks in Egyptian rather than 1,999.

They also have a hard ceiling built into them. Every time you need a bigger number you need a brand-new sign, agreed with everyone else, for the next size up. Roman numerals simply stop: without extra notation there is no way to write 4,000. And the signs tell you nothing about how to calculate — try multiplying MCMXCIX by XLII and see how far the notation helps you.

Problem threeLet the position do the work

The decisive idea appears in Mesopotamia, some time before 2,000 BCE. Instead of giving each size its own sign, give each place its own value. The same handful of marks means one thing in the right-hand column and sixty times as much one column to the left.

This is positional notation, and it changes the economics of writing numbers completely. An additive system needs new signs as numbers grow; a positional one needs new columns, which are free. Two symbols got the Babylonians any number they liked. Ten symbols get us the same.

What a base actually is

The base is just how many a column holds before it has to pass one to the left. Base ten means a column fills at nine; base two means it fills at one; base sixty means it fills at fifty-nine. Nothing else about the machinery changes — which is why a computer using two symbols and a Babylonian scribe using sixty are doing the identical thing.

The missing pieceWhy zero had to be invented

Positional notation has a flaw that shows up immediately: what do you write when a column is empty? The Babylonians left a gap, which is exactly as ambiguous as it sounds. A single wedge might mean one, or sixty, or three thousand six hundred, and readers were expected to work it out from context. Later scribes added a separator sign, but only ever between digits — never at the end, where it was needed most.

The Maya wrote a zero — a shell glyph — and used it as a full placeholder in their vigesimal counts. And in India, by around the seventh century, zero became something more than a gap-filler: a number in its own right, with rules for adding and multiplying it, set out by Brahmagupta. That system travelled west through the Islamic world, where al-Khwārizmī's ninth-century book on calculating with Hindu numerals gave us the word algorithm, and reached Europe in the thirteenth century largely through Fibonacci.

It took centuries to be accepted. Several Italian cities banned the new numerals for official accounts — a 0 is easy to doctor into a 6 or a 9, and a notation nobody's grandfather could read was not obviously trustworthy. Roman numerals stayed in European ledgers long after the mathematics had moved on.

The arbitrary partWhy ten?

Nothing about mathematics prefers ten. Ten is a fact about primates, and the languages give it away: digit means finger, and so does the Latin digitus it comes from.

Other choices left fossils all over daily life. Sixty survives every time you read a clock or measure an angle, inherited directly from Mesopotamia — and sixty is a genuinely good choice, because it divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30, which makes halves and thirds exact. Twelve survives in dozens and inches. Twenty survives in the French quatre-vingts and in score. Two won in the end anyway, because a wire is either carrying current or it is not.

The test of a good notation

Positional notation did not just make numbers shorter to write. It made arithmetic into something you can do mechanically, column by column, without understanding what the number means — which is why long multiplication is teachable to a child, and why the same procedure can be handed to a machine. Additive systems have no such algorithm. That is the whole difference.

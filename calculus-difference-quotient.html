Difference Quotient

Rise Over Run

The steepness of a graph between two points is the rise divided by the run.

\(\Delta\), the Greek letter delta, means “change in”. The run is \(\Delta x\), the change in input, and the rise is \(\Delta y\), the change in output.

Let the first point have input \(x\), and the second have input \(x+h\), where \(h\) is their horizontal separation.

For \(f(x)=x^2\):

\[\Delta y=(x+h)^2-x^2\]

The rise is the gap between the two heights.

Two Strips and a Corner

Expanding:

\[\Delta y=2xh+h^2\]

Each height is the area of a square. Grow the sides of a square from \(x\) to \(x+h\), and \(\Delta y\) is the area it gains.

It gains two strips, each \(x\) by \(h\), and a small corner, \(h\) by \(h\). This is the square of a sum, with the \(x^2\) taken away.

Per Unit of Run

Dividing by the horizontal change:

\[\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}\]

For \(h\neq 0\), we can simplify:

\[\boxed{\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=2x+h}\]

At \(x=2\), this becomes:

\[\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=4+h\]

Dividing by \(h\) gives the rise for a run of \(1\). The straight line through the two points keeps its steepness, so every part of the rise is scaled alike: \(4h\) becomes \(\class{mjx-out}{4}\), and \(h^2\) becomes \(h\).

The same fraction can be made for any function \(f\). It is called the difference quotient.

\[\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\frac{f(x+\class{mjx-in}{h})-f(x)}{\class{mjx-in}{h}}\]

Only \(h=0\) is ruled out. Then the two points are one point, and the fraction is \(\frac{0}{0}\).

What happens as \(h\) closes in on \(0\) is where calculus begins, on Limits.

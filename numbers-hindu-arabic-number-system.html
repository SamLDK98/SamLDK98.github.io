The Hindu-Arabic Number System

36,952

Thirty-six thousand, nine hundred and fifty-two.

This is a single quantity.

It is represented here by five digits, in a particular order.

This representation is a numeral of the Hindu-Arabic number system.

  • Digits are the elementary symbols.
  • Numerals are written representations of numbers.
  • Numbers are the concept of the quantity itself.

Our familiarity with this system hides how impressive a technology it is. To understand how clever it is, let's consider how numbers came to be.

Counting by One-to-One Correspondence

While we lack a detailed account of how the ability to count developed, it's generally believed that physical representations of numbers were used before symbolic ones.

Without relying on some physical apparatus, early humans may only have had a sense of one, two, three, and many.

Tallying might have involved notching marks into bone or tying knots in string to represent the quantity of another group of things.

Alternatively, imagine a shepherd placing a pebble in a bag for each sheep he lets out to graze. When the flock returns, he takes a pebble back out for each sheep he sees. If there are pebbles remaining, he knows he is missing that many sheep.

This type of counting physically implements a one-to-one correspondence, but does not require the counter to possess any mental representation of numbers as they do so.

Notched bones may be the oldest evidence of it we have. The Lebombo bone, a baboon fibula from the border of Eswatini and South Africa, carries 29 notches and is about 44,000 years old. Twenty-nine is close to the days in a lunar month, so it may have been a calendar. A hyena bone from Les Pradelles in France, cut with nine similar notches by Neanderthals around 60,000 years ago, may be older still.

A notch on its own is weak evidence, since it can come from butchery or decoration. The case for counting rests on regularity: notches of one form, evenly spaced, sometimes grouped, like the three columns of marks on the Ishango bone from central Africa, about 20,000 years old. Two of its columns total 60, and one of those holds the primes 11, 13, 17 and 19, though what the marks meant is still argued over.

Oral Counting

Humans probably began with words for quantities of specific things. There may have been a word for three sheep and another word for three apples, but not a word for the abstract idea of three.

English keeps a trace of this. A brace of pheasants and a yoke of oxen are both two, but you cannot have a brace of oxen.

However, at some stage in prehistory, we must have developed spoken words for quantities in the abstract, generalised beyond specific instances.

Written Counting

Numbers were likely among the very first things to be written down.

A notch records how many, but not of what. In the Near East, from about 8,000 BCE, at sites such as Tell Abu Hureyra in Syria and Ganj Dareh in Iran, accounts were kept with small clay tokens. A token's shape gave the commodity, a cone for a measure of grain and a sphere for a larger measure, but the count was still one-to-one: seven measures, seven cones.

In the fourth millennium BCE, the tokens for a deal were sealed inside a clay envelope, and pressed into its surface first, so the contents could be read without breaking it open. The marks now did the job of the tokens. Around 3,300 BCE, scribes at Uruk dropped the tokens and pressed the marks straight into flat tablets. They wrote the quantity with one sign and the commodity with another: five sheep was no longer five sheep-signs, but a sign for five beside a sign for sheep.

We call the early systems sign-value systems.

A sign, or symbol, denotes an abstract quantity: its value.

The simplest sign-value system has one symbol for 1, say a line, and writes every other number as that many lines. This is just a slight extension of the physical one-to-one mapping that preceded it.

Clearly, this still requires the reader to count the lines in their head, using the words they have for the numbers.

At the other extreme, imagine a system where every quantity had its own unique symbol.

Then looking at any symbol would immediately tell you what quantity it represented. That is, if you had memorised the quantity associated with that symbol.

This might be feasible for numbers up to 30 or so, but memorising unique symbols clearly has a low ceiling.

Grouping

Grouping is older than writing. The Věstonice wolf bone, over 30,000 years old, has 55 notches cut in groups of five, so the eye can take the count in chunks. But a group of five strokes is still five strokes.

The breakthrough was to give unique symbols to only some quantities, and build the rest by addition. The Sumerian number signs echo the token shapes: a small cone-shaped mark for 1, a small circle for 10 and a large cone-shaped mark for 60. To write 86 you need only those three symbols, and instead of counting 86 marks you count nine:

86 = 60 + 10 + 10 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1

The adding itself was probably done on fingers or counters.

These are additive systems: the reader adds up the values of the signs. The signs that stand for those fixed values are the system's base symbols.

Egypt, by about 3,000 BCE, used one sign for each power of ten: a stroke for 1, a heel bone for 10, a coiled rope for 100 and a lotus for 1,000. Shang China wrote numbers in tens too, on its oracle bones around 1,200 BCE, and the standard weights of the Indus Valley went up in binary and decimal steps. Ten turns up so often probably because we have ten fingers. Digit still means finger.

The Romans used letters: I for 1, V for 5, X for 10, L for 50, C for 100, D for 500 and M for 1,000. IV is 4 and XC is 90: a smaller numeral just before a larger one is subtracted. The Romans themselves often wrote IIII; the rule was fixed later.

The Greeks of Athens used the first letters of the number words: Π for 5 (pente), Δ for 10 (deka), H for 100 (hekaton) and X for 1,000 (khilioi), with a plain stroke for 1. A Δ written inside a Π meant five tens, 50.

Every sign in an additive numeral has to be written, read and added up.

In an additive system each base symbol has a fixed value: a heel bone means ten wherever it sits. The values are simply added, so, Roman pairs like IV aside, the order of the signs carries no information: HHΔ and ΔHH are both 210. To write larger numbers there are only two options: keep inventing new symbols, or keep repeating the ones you have. In Egyptian, 1,999 takes 28 signs. Roman numerals stop at 3,999; there is no standard way to write 4,000.

Positional Notation

The Hindu-Arabic system does neither. It reuses the same small set of symbols indefinitely, by giving each symbol's value another dimension: its position.

We have a symbol for each of the numbers 1 to 9. To represent ten, instead of creating a new symbol, we start counting groups of ten, and open a new column to hold that count: 10. The 1 now means one group of ten.

But the 1 needs something to its right, or it would just read as one. That is the job of the tenth symbol, 0: it marks a column that is empty, and holds the other digits in their places.

At 20 we have two groups of ten. After 99 the tens column is full too, so we open a third column, for groups of one hundred: 100. In 111, each 1 counts a different size of group: one hundred, one ten and one one.

So in the numeral 352 the digit 3 counts hundreds, the 5 tens and the 2 ones:

352 = 3 × 100 + 5 × 10 + 2 × 1 352 = 3 × 102 + 5 × 101 + 2 × 100

A numeral is still a sum, as in the additive systems, but each term is a digit multiplied by the value of its column. The position of a digit is the power of ten it multiplies.

This is an enormous compression. Egyptian needs ten signs to write 352 and Roman six; we need three. Ten symbols are enough for any number at all, and a number a thousand times larger needs only three more digits.

How many a column can hold before it passes one to the left is the base. Ours is ten, so a column fills at 9. Nothing in the method needs ten: in base two a column fills at 1, which is why computers, built from switches that are either on or off, use it. Twelve has left traces too, in dozens and in the twelve inches of a foot.

Zero

Position was not India's invention. Babylonian scribes used it before 2,000 BCE, in base 60, with just two signs: a wedge for 1 and a corner-shaped wedge for 10, combined to write each digit from 1 to 59. We still count minutes and seconds in sixties.

What they lacked was a zero. An empty column was simply left as a gap, so a single wedge could mean 1, 60 or 3,600, and the reader had to tell from context. Later scribes wrote a separator sign inside numbers, but never at the end, where a gap is invisible.

The Maya wrote a zero, a shell glyph, and used it as a placeholder in their base-20 counts. English still counts in twenties when it says score.

India put the pieces together. By the seventh century, Indian mathematicians wrote numbers with nine digits, place value in base ten, and a zero that was more than a placeholder: a number in its own right, with rules for adding and multiplying it, set out by Brahmagupta in 628.

The system reached the Islamic world, where al-Khwārizmī wrote a book on calculating with Hindu numerals in the ninth century. Europe learned it from Arabic texts, largely through Fibonacci's Liber Abaci of 1202, which is why it carries both names. It was slow to be trusted. In 1299 Florence's money-changers banned the new numerals from their accounts, partly because a 0 is easy to alter into a 6 or a 9. Roman numerals stayed in European ledgers for centuries.

Arithmetic by Columns

The new numerals won in the end because of arithmetic. Try multiplying MCMXCIX by XLII: the signs tell you nothing about how to begin. Positional notation turns arithmetic into a procedure, a fixed set of small steps that works the same way on numbers of any size.

Because every column means a power of ten, two numbers can be lined up by their right-hand ends and each column handled on its own. Adding a column only ever involves two single digits, so you need just one hundred facts, 0 + 0 up to 9 + 9, and one rule for when a column overflows: carry one ten into the next column.

Multiplication works the same way, with the times table. Each digit of the lower number gives one row of the product, set a place further left each time, and the rows are added column by column. Multiplying by ten is just a shift: every digit moves one place left, and a 0 fills the units column.

Additive systems had arithmetic too. To add Egyptian numerals, pool the signs and swap every ten strokes for a heel bone. The Egyptians multiplied by repeated doubling, and Roman merchants calculated on a counting board and wrote down only the result.

What positional notation adds is a method that is uniform, local and bounded. Uniform, because every column is handled the same way. Local, because each step looks at only a couple of digits. Bounded, because the facts you need fit in two ten-by-ten tables, however large the numbers get. That is what lets a child learn long multiplication, and what lets the same procedure run on a machine. The word algorithm comes from al-Khwārizmī's name.

A good notation does some of your thinking for you. The numeral 36,952 lays the structure of the number out where the eye and the pencil can work on it directly: three ten-thousands, six thousands, nine hundreds, five tens and two ones, one column at a time.

The Product-to-Sum Identity

Why does the product of two sinusoidal waves of different frequencies have a long-run average of 0?

Multiplying Two Sine Waves

Take two sine waves with an amplitude of 1 and a phase of 0: one at 2 Hz and one at 3 Hz, observed for one second. In that second, each completes a whole number of cycles.

Multiplying their displacements at every moment gives a product wave:

The same product can be built by adding two cosine waves, each with an amplitude of ½. One has a frequency of 1 Hz, the difference 3 − 2. The other has a frequency of 5 Hz, the sum 2 + 3, and is inverted:

The two constructions give the same wave at every moment. This is true for any two frequencies: the product of two sine waves equals two cosine waves added together, one at the difference of the frequencies and one at their sum.

Why the Average Is 0

A sinusoidal wave has as much displacement above its centre line as below, so over a whole number of cycles its average is 0.

In one second, the 1 Hz and 5 Hz cosine waves complete whole numbers of cycles, so each averages to 0. The product is their sum, so it averages to 0 + 0 = 0.

Over an interval that is not a whole number of cycles, a leftover part of a cycle remains and the average is not exactly 0. The leftover’s effect on the average shrinks as the interval grows, so in the long run the average is 0.

Matching Frequencies

If both waves are at 2 Hz, the difference frequency is 0 Hz. A cosine wave at 0 Hz does not oscillate: it is a constant. The product is a constant ½ plus an inverted 4 Hz cosine wave:

The 4 Hz wave averages to 0. The constant does not, so the product averages to ½.

Why is the product of two sine waves equal to two cosine waves added together?

From Waves to Angles

A sine wave’s displacement at time \(t\) is the sine of an angle that grows steadily with time. For the 2 Hz wave, the angle is \(2\pi\cdot 2t\); for the 3 Hz wave, it is \(2\pi\cdot 3t\).

So the question is about two angles, \(A\) and \(B\): why is \(\sin A \sin B\) made of two cosines, one of \(A - B\) and one of \(A + B\)? The answer comes from rotating a point on a circle.

Rotating a Point

A point on a circle of radius 1, at angle \(A\), has coordinates \((\cos A, \sin A)\). It can be reached from the centre in two steps: a distance \(\cos A\) along the horizontal axis, then a distance \(\sin A\) along the vertical axis.

Now rotate the whole picture, axes and both steps, by a second angle \(B\). The point moves to angle \(A + B\), so its horizontal coordinate is \(\cos(A + B)\). The two steps keep their lengths, \(\cos A\) and \(\sin A\), but now point along the rotated axes.

The horizontal coordinate of the point is the total horizontal distance covered by the two steps. Each step’s horizontal distance is its length multiplied by the horizontal part of its direction.

The first step has length \(\cos A\) and points along the rotated horizontal axis, at angle \(B\). A direction at angle \(B\) has horizontal part \(\cos B\), so this step covers \(\cos A \cos B\).

The second step has length \(\sin A\) and points along the rotated vertical axis, a quarter turn further round, at angle \(B + 90^\circ\). A quarter turn swaps the horizontal and vertical parts of a direction and makes the new horizontal part point backwards: the direction at \(B\) has vertical part \(\sin B\), so the direction at \(B + 90^\circ\) has horizontal part \(-\sin B\). This step covers \(-\sin A \sin B\).

Adding the two:

$$\cos(A + B) = \cos A \cos B - \sin A \sin B$$

Rotating the Other Way

Rotating by \(-B\) instead turns the steps the other way. A direction at \(-B\) has the same horizontal part as one at \(B\), so the first step still covers \(\cos A \cos B\). The second step now points a quarter turn round from \(-B\), and its horizontal part is \(+\sin B\): it points forwards rather than backwards.

$$\cos(A - B) = \cos A \cos B + \sin A \sin B$$

Subtracting the first formula from the second, the \(\cos A \cos B\) terms cancel and the \(\sin A \sin B\) terms add:

$$\cos(A - B) - \cos(A + B) = 2\sin A \sin B$$

Dividing by 2:

$$\sin A \sin B = \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(A - B) - \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(A + B)$$

A product of two sines is two cosines with amplitude ½, at angles \(A - B\) and \(A + B\), the second inverted.

Back to the Waves

Substitute the angles of the 2 Hz and 3 Hz waves, \(A = 2\pi\cdot 2t\) and \(B = 2\pi\cdot 3t\):

$$A - B = 2\pi\cdot 2t - 2\pi\cdot 3t = -2\pi\cdot 1t$$

$$A + B = 2\pi\cdot 2t + 2\pi\cdot 3t = 2\pi\cdot 5t$$

$$\sin(2\pi\cdot 2t)\,\sin(2\pi\cdot 3t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(-2\pi\cdot 1t) - \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(2\pi\cdot 5t)$$

Reversing the sign of a cosine’s angle leaves it unchanged, because the cosine is the horizontal coordinate, and turning by \(-x\) instead of \(x\) gives the same horizontal coordinate. So \(\cos(-2\pi\cdot 1t) = \cos(2\pi\cdot 1t)\): the 1 Hz wave. The angle \(A + B\) grows at 5 Hz: the inverted 5 Hz wave.

For any two frequencies \(f_1\) and \(f_2\):

$$\sin(2\pi f_1 t)\,\sin(2\pi f_2 t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\cos\big(2\pi (f_1 - f_2) t\big) - \tfrac{1}{2}\cos\big(2\pi (f_1 + f_2) t\big)$$

This is why multiplying by a test wave picks out a single frequency in the Fourier Transform Visualisation.

Other amplitudes, phases, intervals, and sine times cosine

Amplitude. Waves with amplitudes \(a\) and \(b\) scale both cosine waves by \(ab\). Different frequencies still average to 0.

Phase. Changing the phase of either wave shifts the phases of the two cosine waves, but not their frequencies, so different frequencies still average to 0. For matching frequencies, the constant becomes \(\tfrac{1}{2}ab\cos(\varphi_1 - \varphi_2)\): \(\tfrac{1}{2}ab\) when the phases match, and 0 when they differ by a quarter of a cycle.

Interval. The cancellation is exact only over a whole number of cycles of both the difference and sum waves. Otherwise a leftover part of a cycle remains; its effect on the average shrinks as the interval grows.

Sine times cosine. Reading off the vertical coordinate in the same way gives \(\sin(A + B) = \sin A \cos B + \cos A \sin B\) and \(\sin(A - B) = \sin A \cos B - \cos A \sin B\). Adding them gives \(\sin A \cos B = \tfrac{1}{2}\sin(A + B) + \tfrac{1}{2}\sin(A - B)\): again two sinusoidal waves, at the sum and difference frequencies, so the average is 0. For matching frequencies, \(\sin(A - B) = \sin 0 = 0\), so the average is 0 even then. A sine wave and a cosine wave at the same frequency never leave a constant.

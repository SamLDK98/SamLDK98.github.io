Two Tests, Two Passes

Here is a quadratic equation:

\[x^2-5x+6=0\]

(It's the same equation as \(x^2-5x=-6\), with everything moved to one side.)

It has two solutions: \(2\) and \(3\).

Whatever number we test goes into every \(x\) at once:

\[2^2-5(2)+6=0\qquad3^2-5(3)+6=0\]

\(x\) is never \(2\) in one place and \(3\) in another. These are two separate tests, and each one passes.

So how can two different numbers pass a test with only one unknown?

One answer: the equation is a question about two numbers, in disguise.

A Question About Two Numbers

Find two numbers that add to \(5\) and multiply to \(6\).

With two unknowns, that is:

\[x+y=5\qquad xy=6\]

On its own, \(x+y=5\) has endless solutions. Adding \(xy=6\) narrows them to two pairs: \((2,3)\) and \((3,2)\). The same two numbers, in either order.

The two meeting points are mirror images across the diagonal: each is the other with its coordinates swapped. Raise the product, and they stay mirror images until they meet on the diagonal and vanish. We'll see why below.

Now hide \(y\). The first equation says \(y=5-x\). Put that into the second:

\[\begin{aligned} x(5-x)&=6\\ 5x-x^2&=6\\ x^2-5x+6&=0 \end{aligned}\]

That's our quadratic. It has one unknown, but it asks the same question. Asking for \(x\) now means asking: what could one of the two numbers be? There are two right answers, \(2\) and \(3\).

Hiding \(y\) didn't make the second number go away. It turned up as the second solution.

Every Quadratic Asks for a Pair

But a quadratic we're handed doesn't come with a \(y\). Does it still hide a pair?

It does. Start from any two numbers, \(r\) and \(s\). The equation

\[(x-r)(x-s)=0\]

is true exactly when \(x=r\) or \(x=s\), because a product is zero only when one of its factors is zero.

Multiply it out:

\[x^2-(r+s)x+rs=0\]

The coefficients are the sum and the product of the two solutions. For \(2\) and \(3\), that's \(x^2-5x+6=0\).

So, read backwards, every quadratic asks: which two numbers have this sum and this product? For \(x^2-5x+6=0\), the sum is \(5\) and the product is \(6\). (If the \(x^2\) term has a coefficient, divide through by it first.)

The Equation Can't Tell Them Apart

A sum and a product don't care about order: \(2+3=3+2\) and \(2\times3=3\times2\). So nothing in \(x^2-5x+6=0\) can prefer \(2\) over \(3\). In the builder above, drag one solution past the other: the last line doesn't change.

We can check this directly with the form we found earlier, \(x(5-x)=6\). Call \(5-x\) the partner of \(x\): the number that brings the sum up to \(5\). Swapping a number for its partner gives the same product, so whenever a number passes, its partner passes too.

Solutions come in pairs. The only number that is its own partner is \(2.5\).

Finding the Pair

To solve \(x^2-5x+6=0\), look for two numbers with sum \(5\) and product \(6\).

Two numbers that add to \(5\) have an average of \(2.5\), so they sit the same distance either side of it: \(2.5-d\) and \(2.5+d\).

Their product is:

\[(2.5-d)(2.5+d)=6.25-d^2\]

For a product of \(6\), \(d^2=0.25\), so \(d=0.5\). The numbers are \(2\) and \(3\).

The product is largest when \(d=0\), and then it's \(6.25\). That decides how many solutions there are:

  • Below \(6.25\): two different numbers, so two solutions.
  • Exactly \(6.25\): the pair is \(2.5\) and \(2.5\). One solution.
  • Above \(6.25\): no pair exists. \(x^2-5x+7=0\) has no real solutions. (The complex numbers are where equations like this find theirs.)

That's why, in the first picture, the meeting points merge on the diagonal at \(6.25\) and then vanish.

More Solutions Built In

A linear equation is built from one solution: \(2x-3=0\) is \(2(x-1.5)=0\).

A cubic is built from three: \((x-1)(x-2)(x-4)=0\) multiplies out to \(x^3-7x^2+14x-8=0\), with solutions \(1\), \(2\) and \(4\).

An equation of degree \(n\) has at most \(n\) solutions.

Back to the Puzzle

\(x\) is never two numbers at once. A quadratic is a question about two numbers, written with only one symbol. Both numbers fit the question equally well, so each one, tested on its own, passes.

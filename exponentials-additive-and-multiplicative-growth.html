Exponentials

Additive and Multiplicative Growth

Understanding the difference between additive and multiplicative growth is a key prerequisite for understanding exponentiation.

In additive growth, each step adds the same amount to the current output.

In multiplicative growth, each step multiplies the current output by the same factor.

Let's visualise growing by a constant factor of \(\class{mjx-base}{4}\).

The grid represents the factor we are increasing by. It could have any number of cells to represent any factor, but here it was four. We can see that although the absolute increase from one square to the next varies, the next square is always \(4\) times as big as the previous one.

Progress in Additive Journeys

Say we start at:

\[0\]

We want to reach:

\[256\]

Rather than jumping directly to \(256\), imagine travelling there, passing through all the intermediate values.

Although we can think of this journey as continuous, we can also divide it into discrete steps.

For an additive journey, the distance (i.e. the change in output) covered by each step must be equal.

Therefore, we can divide the journey into as many or as few steps as we wish, as long as we adjust the step size accordingly.

For a target output, through division, we can find:

  • The number of steps we must take (given a step size)
\[\frac{\text{target output}}{\text{step size}} = \text{total steps in the journey}\]
\[\text{a) }\frac{200}{2} = 100 \text{ equally spaced steps}\]
\[\text{b) }\frac{200}{50} = 4 \text{ equally spaced steps}\]
\[\text{c) }\frac{200}{5} = 40 \text{ equally spaced steps}\]
  • The step size (given a number of steps)
\[\frac{\text{target output}}{\text{total steps in the journey}} = \text{step size}\]
\[\text{a) }\frac{200}{100} = 2 \text{ (step size)}\]
\[\text{b) }\frac{200}{4} = 50 \text{ (step size)}\]
\[\text{c) }\frac{200}{40} = 5 \text{ (step size)}\]

Equal Numbers of Additive Steps Produce Equal Changes in the Output

In additive growth, every step changes the output by the same amount. Consequently, the difference between successive outputs is always the same (regardless of where we are in the journey).

For example, suppose each step adds \(6\) to the current output:

\[0 \to 6 \to 12 \to 18 \to 24 \to 30 \to 36 \to 42 \to 48\]
\[\text{Step 2} - \text{Step 1}: \quad 12 - 6 = \boxed{6}\]
\[\text{Step 6} - \text{Step 5}: \quad 36 - 30 = \boxed{6}\]

Further, if we take \(x\) steps, the difference between the initial output and the final output will be the same. The change accumulates. Here, \(3\) steps covers a change of \(6 + 6 + 6 = 18\) in the output.

For example:

\[\text{Step 4} - \text{Step 1}: \quad 24 - 6 = \boxed{18}\]
\[\text{Step 8} - \text{Step 5}: \quad 48 - 30 = \boxed{18}\]

The Relationship Between Additive Steps and Output

We can track two quantities as we progress in our growth journey:

  • Steps taken
  • Corresponding output

Because each step increases the output by the same amount:

\[\text{steps taken }\% = \text{target output }\%\]

Imagine the target output is \(200\).

We divide our journey into:

\[\text{a) } 100 \text{ steps of } +2\]
\[\text{b) } 4 \text{ steps of } +50\]
\[\text{c) } 40 \text{ steps of } +5\]

When we have taken \(25\%\) of our steps:

\[\text{a) } 25\]
\[\text{b) } 1\]
\[\text{c) } 10\]

We will have covered \(25\%\) of the output:

\[\text{a) } 25 \times 2 = 50\]
\[\text{b) } 1 \times 50 = 50\]
\[\text{c) } 10 \times 5 = 50\]

Reconstructing the Total from Partial Progress

When a percentage divides evenly into \(100\%\), reconstructing the total output is straightforward.

For example, if we know the output after completing \(25\%\) of our steps is \(x\), then the target output must be \(4x\), because four groups of \(25\%\) make up the complete journey.

But what if we have completed \(40\%\) of our steps and reached an output of \(80\)?

\[40\% \text{ steps taken} \to \text{current output} = 80\]

We can calculate how many groups of \(40\%\) make up \(100\%\):

\[\frac{100}{40} = 2.5\]

Therefore, we multiply our current output by \(2.5\):

\[80 \times 2.5 = \boxed{200}\]

The same approach works for percentages greater than \(50\%\).

Suppose we have completed \(85\%\) of our steps and reached an output of \(170\).

\[\frac{100}{85} = \frac{20}{17} \approx 1.17647\]

Therefore:

\[170 \times \frac{20}{17} = \boxed{200}\]

We can generalise this relationship: if we know the output reached after completing a given percentage of an additive journey starting at zero, we can recover the target by multiplying that output by the number of times the completed percentage fits into \(100\%\).

Progress in Multiplicative Journeys

Multiplicative journeys start at \(1\) instead of \(0\).

Say we start at:

\[1\]

We want to reach:

\[256\]

This time we progress by multiplying the current output by a constant factor.

Imagine we always multiply the current output by \(\times\class{mjx-base}{2}\):

\[1 \to 2 \to 4 \to 8 \to 16 \to 32 \to 64 \to 128 \to 256\]

As before, we can track:

  • Steps taken
  • Corresponding output

Unlike additive growth, the differences between successive outputs are not equal:

\[\text{step 2} - \text{step 1: }\ 4 - 2 = \boxed{2}\]
\[\text{step 6} - \text{step 5: }\ 64 - 32 = \boxed{32}\]

However, the ratios between them are:

\[\frac{\text{step 2}}{\text{step 1}} \to\ \frac{4}{2} = \boxed{2}\]
\[\frac{\text{step 6}}{\text{step 5}} \to\ \frac{64}{32} = \boxed{2}\]

Recall that in additive growth, the changes produced by successive steps accumulate through addition. For example, three steps of \(+6\) produce a total increase of:

\[6 + 6 + 6 = 18\]

In multiplicative growth, the factors accumulate through multiplication.

Three steps of \(\times 2\) produce a total multiplicative change of:

\[2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8\]

Therefore, equal numbers of multiplicative steps produce equal ratios between outputs, regardless of where they occur along the journey.

For example:

\[\frac{\text{step 4}}{\text{step 1}} \to\ \frac{16}{2} = \boxed{8}\]
\[\frac{\text{step 8}}{\text{step 5}} \to\ \frac{256}{32} = \boxed{8}\]

The Relationship Between Multiplicative Steps and Output

When each step changes the output by a constant factor:

\[\text{steps taken }\% \ne \text{target output }\%\]

Previously, we used division to identify the number of steps needed to reach our target output given a constant step size.

Now, we will use logarithms to identify the number of steps needed to reach our target output given a constant step ratio (we will discuss this operation in more detail later).

Target: \(256\)
Step size ratio: \(\times\class{mjx-base}{2}\)

\[\log_{2}(256) = 8\]

Number of steps in the journey from \(1 \to 256\): \(8\).

\[1 \to 2 \to 4 \to 8 \to 16 \to 32 \to 64 \to 128 \to 256\]

Let's examine the relationship between the percentage of steps completed and the percentage of the target output reached.

% of Steps Taken% of Output ReachedOutput
\(25\%\)\(\approx 1.56\%\)\(4\)
\(40\%\)\(\approx 3.59\%\)\(\approx 9.19\)
\(50\%\)\(6.25\%\)\(16\)
\(85\%\)\(\approx 43.53\%\)\(\approx 111.43\)
\(100\%\)\(100\%\)\(256\)

Recall that, in additive growth, equal portions of a journey contribute equal increases in output. For example, completing \(25\%\) of the steps in a journey from \(0\) to \(200\) produces an output of \(50\).

Because four groups of \(25\%\) make up the complete journey, we can add the accumulated increase four times:

\[50 + 50 + 50 + 50 = 200\]

In multiplicative growth, equal portions of a journey contribute equal multiplicative factors instead.

After completing \(25\%\) of our steps, we have reached \(4\).

That is an increase of \(\times 4\) from our starting output \(1\).

Each subsequent \(25\%\) of the journey must then multiply the current output by that same factor of \(4\):

\[1 \xrightarrow{\times 4} 4 \xrightarrow{\times 4} 16 \xrightarrow{\times 4} 64 \xrightarrow{\times 4} 256\]

Therefore:

\[4 \times 4 \times 4 \times 4 = 256\]

Because four groups of \(25\%\) make up the complete journey:

\[\frac{100}{25} = 4\]

We can express the repeated multiplication using an exponent:

\[4^4 = \boxed{256}\]

If we took \(50\%\) of our steps, we calculate how many groups of \(50\%\) make up the journey:

\[\frac{100}{50} = 2\]

The output after \(50\%\) is \(16\).

So to reach \(100\%\) we must multiply this value by itself \(2\) times:

\[16 \times 16\]
\[16^2\]
\[16 \times 16 = 256\]

Taking \(\frac{1}{2}\) our steps means we can multiply the value there by itself to reach the output because the ratio between the start and the halfway point must be the same as the ratio between the halfway point and the final target output.

What about \(40\%\)?

\[\frac{100}{40} = 2.5\]

Our multiplicative factor has accumulated \(2.5\) times.

\[\approx 9.19^{2.5}\]

Finally, consider \(85\%\) (\(\frac{17}{20}\)) of steps complete.

\[\frac{100}{85} = 1.17647059\]
\[111.43^{1.17647059}\]

The difference is that with additive growth, we multiply; with multiplicative growth, we exponentiate.

Suppose we have completed \(25\%\) of our steps and reached an output of \(x\).

In an additive journey starting at \(0\), the increases accumulate through addition:

\[x + x + x + x = \text{Target output}\]

Which we can express as multiplication:

\[\boxed{4x = \text{Target output}}\]

In a multiplicative journey starting at \(1\), the factors accumulate through multiplication:

\[x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x = \text{Target output}\]

Which we can express as exponentiation:

\[\boxed{x^4 = \text{Target output}}\]

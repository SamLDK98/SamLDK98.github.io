Why Quadratics are Tricky

One Unknown, Two Solutions?

Equations with one unknown usually have one solution. In \(3x-1=5\), \(x\) can only be \(2\).

With two unknowns there can be infinitely many. In \(3x+2y=5\), \(x\) can be anything at all, and for each choice there is exactly one \(y\) that balances it.

So \(x^2-5x=6\), which has one unknown but two solutions, \(-1\) and \(6\), feels counterintuitive. Let's build up from the simplest equations and see where the second solution comes from.

Adding Copies of \(x\)

\(x=9\) has one solution. So does \(x+x=9\): \(x\) must be \(4.5\), because no other number doubled gives \(9\). The same is true of \(x+x+x=9\), \(x+x+x+x=9\), and so on.

Anything that simplifies to a whole number of copies of \(x\) behaves the same way:

\[\begin{aligned} 4x=9\quad&\rightarrow\quad x+x+x+x=9\\ 2x+x=9\quad&\rightarrow\quad x+x+x=9\\ 3\cdot2x=9\quad&\rightarrow\quad 6x=9,\ \text{six copies of }x \end{aligned}\]

Each copy of \(x\) grows when \(x\) grows, so the total only ever grows. It passes \(9\) once.

A Constant Times an Unknown

Multiplication is often introduced as repeated addition. But with negative numbers, it treats signs differently.

Adding:

  • \((+)+(+)=(+)\)
  • \((-)+(-)=(-)\)
  • \((+)+(-)\) takes the sign of whichever number is larger in size (or is \(0\), if they're the same size).

Multiplying:

  • \((+)\cdot(+)=(+)\)
  • \((-)\cdot(-)=(+)\)
  • \((+)\cdot(-)=(-)\), whatever the sizes.

Multiplying by a negative always flips the sign. That is why two negatives make a positive.

In a linear equation the constant is known. Take \(-2x=8\). The output is positive and the constant is negative, so the table says \(x\) must be negative. Its size is \(8\div2=4\). So \(x=-4\): dividing by \(-2\) undoes the multiplication, and there is only one way to undo it.

\(8\) can be made in many ways, such as \(2\cdot4\) or \((-2)(-4)\), but one factor is fixed at \(-2\), so only one partner works.

An Unknown Times an Unknown

Compare:

\[x+x=9\qquad x\cdot x=9\]

In both, the two numbers being combined are the same number, so they have the same sign.

For addition, \((+)+(+)=(+)\) and \((-)+(-)=(-)\): the sign of the sum tells us the sign of \(x\), and the size then follows. So \(x+x=9\) has one solution.

For multiplication, \((+)(+)=(+)\) and \((-)(-)=(+)\): both signs give a positive output, so the output can't tell us the sign of \(x\). Its size must be \(3\), so \(x=3\) or \(x=-3\).

Multiplying \(x\) by itself erases its sign. That's why \(x^2=9\) has a pair of opposite solutions.

But \(x^2-5x=6\) has solutions \(-1\) and \(6\), which aren't opposites. To see how, we need to understand two things: why \(x^2\) and \(x\) terms can't be combined, and how the output changes as \(x\) changes.

Why \(x^2\) and \(x\) Can't Be Combined

Like terms combine because they are copies of the same thing: \(2x+3x=5x\).

\(5x\) is five copies of \(x\), whatever \(x\) is. But \(x\cdot x\) is \(x\) copies of \(x\): three copies of \(3\), or four copies of \(4\). The number of copies is the unknown itself.

No fixed number of copies works for every \(x\). If \(x^2\) were \(Ax\), then at \(x=1\) we would need \(A=1\), and at \(x=2\) we would need \(A=2\).

So \(x^2\) and \(5x\) are different kinds of term, and \(x^2-5x\) stays a mix of the two. Each kind changes the output in its own way as \(x\) grows, and the mix adds those two changes together. That is what lets the two solutions be different numbers, rather than a pair of opposites.

The change between two squares next to each other is the sum of the two numbers: from \(3^2\) to \(4^2\) the output rises by \(3+4=7\), and from \((-3)^2\) to \((-2)^2\) it changes by \(-3+(-2)=-5\). (Multiply out: \((a+1)^2-a^2=2a+1\), which is \(a+(a+1)\).)

So the output falls while that sum is negative, and rises once it is positive. The sum switches sign as the numbers cross \(0\). And each step, both numbers are \(1\) bigger than last time, so the change is \(2\) bigger: the change of change is always \(+2\).

The output falls, turns once at \(0\), and rises. Every output above the turn is reached twice: once on the way down, once on the way up. The changes either side of the turn mirror each other (\(-1,-3,-5\) and \(+1,+3,+5\)), so the two inputs sit the same distance either side of \(0\).

The change of change is still \(+2\). How fast the change grows has stayed exactly the same.

That is because the two kinds of term change the output in different ways. The \(x^2\) part changes by the sum of the two numbers, as before. The \(-5x\) part takes away \(5\) on every step, the same every time. And the \(-6\) never changes. So each change is just \(x^2\)'s change minus \(5\), and the changes still grow by \(2\) each step.

The output still falls, turns once and rises, with the same shape as \(x^2\). But the changes now reach \(0\) later, between \(2\) and \(3\): the turning point has moved to \(2.5\).

Change the linear term and watch the rows: every change shifts by the same amount, while the change of change stays \(+2\).

The outputs mirror around \(2.5\): \(2\) and \(3\) both give \(-12\), \(1\) and \(4\) both give \(-10\), \(0\) and \(5\) both give \(-6\), and \(-1\) and \(6\) both give \(0\).

That is how \(x^2-5x=6\) ends up with the solutions \(-1\) and \(6\): completely different numbers, but each \(3.5\) from the turning point.

Why Quadratics are Tricky

In a linear equation, the unknown is only added to itself or multiplied by known numbers. The output changes by the same amount every step, so it passes each value once.

In a quadratic, the unknown is multiplied by itself. That erases its sign, and makes the change itself change. The output falls, turns and rises, so it can pass a value twice.

A linear term can't merge with \(x^2\). It shifts every change by the same amount, which moves the turning point. The two solutions are mirror images around that point, not around \(0\).

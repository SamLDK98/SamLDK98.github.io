Basics of Electricity

Everything else in this section — equilibrium potentials, action potentials, synapses — is bookkeeping about charge and the energy it carries. This page lays down that groundwork from the beginning.

Part oneCharge

Charge ($Q$) is a fundamental property of subatomic particles.

  • Protons carry a single unit of positive elementary charge ($+1e$).
  • Electrons carry a single unit of negative elementary charge ($-1e$).

While charge is measured in coulombs ($\text{C}$), the amount of charge carried by subatomic particles is a tiny fraction of one coulomb, which we call an elementary charge ($e$).

Atoms that have a positive or negative charge are called ions.

  • Atoms that have fewer electrons than protons have a positive charge and are called cations.
  • Atoms that have fewer protons than electrons have a negative charge and are called anions.

There is a rule governing the movement of cations and anions: they move towards their opposites and away from their likes.

Part twoElectric potential and voltage

Let's say we have two compartments in a box.

  • In compartment 1, $Q = +4e$.
  • In compartment 2, $Q = -6e$.

All charge tells us is the ratio of $+$ to $-$.

We also need to know how much electric potential energy each unit of charge is to be multiplied by to know the electric potential. But what is electric potential energy, and how do we calculate it?

Electric potential energy ($U$) is the form of potential energy that a charged particle holds due to its position relative to other charged particles in an electric field.

Just like a compressed spring holds elastic potential energy, a set of arranged charges holds electric potential energy. If you let them go, that stored energy converts into kinetic energy as opposite charges accelerate toward each other (or like charges snap apart).

The formula for potential energy is:

$$\large U = k_e \frac{q_1 q_2}{r}$$
  • Distance ($r$): The closer charges get to each other, the stronger the force and the higher the magnitude of potential energy.
  • Charges ($q_1, q_2$): Larger charges produce stronger attractions or repulsions, yielding more energy.
  • The medium ($\varepsilon_r$): The $k_e$ term contains the permittivity of the environment. In a vacuum, $k_e$ is constant. However, in water or biological cells, the surrounding molecules screen the charges, dividing $k_e$ by a factor of about 80 and drastically reducing the potential energy compared to a vacuum.

In order to calculate the electric potential energy, we need to know:

  • The difference in charge between the two compartments.
  • The distance between the compartments.
  • Some information about the surrounding material.

Why the medium matters so much

That factor of 80 is not a detail — it is the reason cells work. In the figure, two opposite ions 1 nm apart hold about $-1.44\ \text{eV}$ in a vacuum, but only about $-18\ \text{meV}$ in water. Random thermal jostling at room temperature carries roughly $25\ \text{meV}$ — the same $\tfrac{k_BT}{e} \approx 25\ \text{mV}$ that sets the scale of the Nernst equation. So in water, the pull between neighbouring ions is weaker than the thermal noise, and ions are free to wander. In a vacuum they would be locked together.

Laws of Exponents

As the exponent increases additively, the corresponding value of the power increases multiplicatively.

The step size for the exponent's journey is just \(1\).

The step ratio for the power's journey depends on the base.

Imagine a step ratio of \(\times 2\):

Adding exponents in the top row corresponds to multiplying values in the bottom row.

Product of Powers Rule\(a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}\)

A power is just notation for an idea we previously saw: multiplicative steps combine their factors through multiplication.

\(2^3\) is just \(3\) steps of \(\times 2\) \(\rightarrow\) a combined factor of \(2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = \times 8\).

\(2^5\) is just \(5\) steps of \(\times 2\) \(\rightarrow\) a combined factor of \(2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = \times 32\).

When we see a power written as \(b^n\), we can assume the journey begins at \(1\) and the multiplicative factor is being applied to \(1\).

\[1 \cdot b^n\]

Notice that as we increase the number of multiplicative steps by \(1\):

  • We add \(1\) to the exponent.
  • We multiply our output by another copy of the base.

The power does not have to be applied to \(1\). We could move it up and down the number line and include a coefficient to represent the initial value that the power applies to.

Multiplying two powers can be thought of like this. One power applies to \(1\) to give us the output that the other power applies to.

For example:

\[2^3 \cdot 2^5\]
\[2^3 \rightarrow 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8\]

The first power is applied to \(1\) and takes us to \(8\).

The second power, which represents a combined factor of \(\times 32\), is then applied to \(8\).

\[8 \cdot 2^5\]
\[8 \cdot 32 = 256\]

It's not necessary to think like this when performing the calculations. It's as simple as:

\[2^3 \cdot 2^5 = 8 \cdot 32 = 256\]

Notice that if we rewrite the product as a power of base \(2\):

\[256 = 2^8\]

We see that multiplying powers together gives the same value as adding their exponents:

\[2^3 \cdot 2^5 = 2^{3+5} = 2^8\]

Quotient of Powers Rule\(\dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n} \quad (a \ne 0)\)

Previously we saw that taking a multiplicative step added \(1\) to the exponent and multiplied the output by the base.

Taking a step backwards therefore subtracts \(1\) from the exponent and divides the output by the base.

Dividing a power by a power is therefore the same as subtracting the exponent in the denominator from the exponent in the numerator.

We are just taking that number of steps backwards.

The matching factors in the numerator and denominator cancel.

Power of a Power Rule\((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\)

We've seen that a power just represents the combined factors of successive steps.

We could think of \(2^3\) as either:

  • Three steps of \(\times 2\).
  • One step of \(\times 8\).

Raising the power to a power just repeats the entire original product. The total number of factors is therefore the product of the two exponents.

Power of a Product Rule\((ab)^n = a^n b^n\)

Raising a product to a power is equivalent to raising each factor to that power and then multiplying the results together.

Power of a Quotient Rule\(\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^n = \dfrac{a^n}{b^n} \quad (b \ne 0)\)

Raising a quotient to a power is equivalent to raising the numerator and denominator separately to that power.

For example:

\[\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^3\]

This means multiplying three copies of the fraction together:

\[\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\]

When multiplying fractions, we multiply their numerators together and their denominators together:

\[\frac{2\cdot2\cdot2}{3\cdot3\cdot3}\]

Therefore:

\[\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^3=\frac{2^3}{3^3}=\frac{8}{27}\]

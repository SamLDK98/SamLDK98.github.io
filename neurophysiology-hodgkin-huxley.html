The Hodgkin–Huxley Model

In 1952 Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley wrote down four coupled differential equations that reproduce the action potential from first principles. Here is the current clamp they used. See what the axon will do.

The setupA wire made of seawater

A neuron is a terrible cable. Its interior is salty water, which conducts electricity roughly ten million times worse than copper, wrapped in a leaky membrane. A voltage injected at one end of a long axon should fade to nothing within a few millimetres.

It does not. Signals travel a metre down your leg without losing amplitude, because the axon does not passively carry the signal — it regenerates it, over and over, using energy stored in the ion gradients across its own membrane. The question Hodgkin and Huxley set themselves was how.

They worked on the giant axon of the squid, which at up to a millimetre across is fat enough to push a wire down. That let them clamp the membrane at a chosen voltage and measure the current that flowed in response — and, crucially, to swap the seawater outside for solutions with different sodium concentrations, isolating one ion at a time.

The ideaThe membrane is a circuit

Their central move was to stop thinking about biology and start thinking about electronics. A patch of membrane is a capacitor: a thin insulating lipid layer with charge piled up on either side. In parallel with it sit three conductances — one for sodium, one for potassium, and a small constant leak for everything else.

Each conductance has its own battery: the equilibrium potential at which that ion stops moving, set by its concentration gradient. Sodium is concentrated outside the cell, so ENa sits high, around +50 mV. Potassium is concentrated inside, so EK sits low, around −77 mV. Current through each branch is just Ohm's law, and the whole thing is Kirchhoff's current law:

Cm dVdt = I − ḡNa m3h (V − ENa) − ḡK n4 (V − EK) − ḡL (V − EL)

the rate the membrane charges = injected current, minus everything leaking back out through the three conductances

Everything interesting is hiding in those conductances. If ḡNa and ḡK were constants, this would be a dull leaky capacitor and nothing would ever spike. The whole phenomenon lives in the fact that the conductances depend on the voltage they help produce.

The gatesThree knobs, turning at different speeds

Hodgkin and Huxley had no idea ion channels existed — the structures would not be seen for another thirty years. So they did something bolder than guessing: they described the conductances phenomenologically, as if each were controlled by a number of independent gates, each flipping between open and shut with voltage-dependent rates.

Three variables do all the work. Each is a probability between 0 and 1, and each obeys the same simple form — relaxing toward a voltage-dependent target with a voltage-dependent time constant:

dxdt = αx(V) (1 − x) − βx(V) x     for x = m, h, n

α is the rate of opening, β the rate of closing; both are functions of voltage alone

The exponents are the trick. Sodium conductance goes as m3h and potassium as n4, because the data demanded curves with a delayed, S-shaped onset, which is what you get when several independent gates must all be open at once. That was a fitting decision. It turned out to be very nearly a structural prediction: the potassium channel really does have four subunits.

What each gate does

  • m — sodium activation. Very fast. Opens within a fraction of a millisecond when the membrane depolarises. This is the positive feedback loop: depolarisation opens m, sodium rushes in, which depolarises further, which opens more m. Once it starts it cannot be stopped.
  • h — sodium inactivation. Slow, and runs backwards: depolarisation makes it close. It is the brake that was already being applied before the accelerator was pressed. Within a millisecond or two it shuts the sodium conductance down regardless of voltage.
  • n — potassium activation. Slow to open. Potassium flows outward, dragging the membrane back down — and because n is also slow to close, it overshoots past rest, which is the after-hyperpolarisation.

An action potential is what those three timescales produce when you let them interact: a fast one that fires, a slow one that stops it, and another slow one that resets it.

ExperimentsFour things worth doing

The panels are stacked so you can read causes above effects: membrane potential on top, the two conductances beneath it, the gates that set those conductances below that, and the current you are injecting at the bottom.

01 — threshold Find the all-or-none point

Set the pulse to 1 ms and start at 5 µA/cm². Nothing — a small passive bump. Creep upward. Between 6.7 and 7.0 the response stops growing gradually and jumps to a full 100 mV spike. There is nothing in between, and pushing to 40 barely makes the spike bigger.

02 — refractoriness Try to fire twice

Fire a spike, then hammer the button. At the default 12 µA/cm² you need to wait about 14 ms before a second one will go; a 20 µA/cm² pulse gets in at 11, but nothing gets in before 10. Watch the h trace: it has collapsed toward zero and has to climb back before sodium can conduct again. That recovery is what caps a neuron's firing rate.

03 — repetitive firing Hold the current on

Raise the holding current slowly from zero. Below about 6 µA/cm² the cell sits still; just above it you get a burst that dies out. By 6.5 it fires forever — and not fading in from 0 Hz, but switching on abruptly at roughly 55 Hz and climbing from there. Rate codes intensity, but only over that window.

04 — blockers Poison the channels

TTX, the pufferfish toxin, plugs the sodium channel: spikes vanish entirely and you are left with a passive membrane. TEA blocks potassium instead — the cell fires, then cannot get back down, and sticks near 0 mV. Repolarisation is not automatic; it is actively done by potassium.

The parametersWhat the numbers are

These are the standard values, in the modern sign convention where the resting potential is −65 mV and depolarisation is positive. Hodgkin and Huxley's original paper used the opposite convention, measuring displacement from rest with inward current positive, which is why their figures look upside down next to a modern textbook's.

At rest the gates settle at m = 0.053, h = 0.596 and n = 0.318. Sodium is almost fully shut but ready; the brake is more than half off; potassium is partly open. The three currents cancel to within a rounding error, which is what makes rest an equilibrium rather than a resting place.

The simulation integrates with forward Euler at a fixed 0.01 ms step, independent of your screen's frame rate. That step size matters: the upstroke of a spike is fast enough that a coarser step visibly distorts it, and a much coarser one diverges outright.

Why it matteredA model that predicted its own mechanism

Hodgkin and Huxley fitted their equations by hand, then integrated them on a mechanical calculator — a process that took about three weeks per spike. What came out was not just a curve that matched their data. It reproduced the conduction velocity of the axon, the threshold, the refractory period, and the after-hyperpolarisation, none of which had been fitted for.

They were careful to say the gates were a description, not a claim about machinery. But the description was constraining enough that when ion channels were finally isolated, they looked remarkably like what the equations required: discrete, voltage-sensitive, multi-subunit, with separate activation and inactivation. The Nobel came in 1963. The equations are still what anyone means by a neuron when they sit down to simulate one.

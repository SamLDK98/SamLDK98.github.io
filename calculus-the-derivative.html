Calculus

The Derivative

Slope Needs Two Points

Slope measures steepness: the rise divided by the run between two points.

A curve's steepness changes as you move along it, so its slope has to be found at a single point. But a single point has no run.

The way round this was on Limits from the start. Its first function was a slope.

\[\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}\]

It is the rise over the run from \((1,1)\) to a second point on the curve \(y=x^2\), at \((x,x^2)\). Write \(x\) as \(1+h\) and it becomes the slope Limits ended with.

The line through two points of a curve is called a secant.

At \(x=1\) the two points are one point, and the slope is \(\frac{0}{0}\). Nearby, the slopes were \(\class{mjx-out}{1.9},\ \class{mjx-out}{1.99},\ \class{mjx-out}{1.999}\) from below and \(\class{mjx-out}{2.1},\ \class{mjx-out}{2.01}\) from above.

From both sides, the secants close in on one line through \((1,1)\), with slope \(\class{mjx-out}{2}\).

That line is the tangent. Its slope is what we mean by the slope of the curve at that point.

Straight Up Close

There is a second way to find the tangent. Zoom in on \((1,1)\), and the curve itself straightens out.

Close enough, the curve and its tangent cannot be told apart.

This is what makes a line a tangent: not touching the curve once, but matching it up close.

A straight line looks the same at every zoom. It is its own tangent, with the same slope everywhere.

A Slope at Every Point

Nothing above needed the first point to be at \(1\). Put it at any \(x\), and the second point a distance \(h\) away, at \(x+h\).

\[\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}=\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}=2x+h\]

As \(h\) closes in on \(0\), the slope closes in on \(2x\).

So the slope of \(x^2\) at \(x\) is \(2x\): \(\class{mjx-out}{-2}\) at \(x=\class{mjx-in}{-1}\), \(\class{mjx-out}{0}\) at the bottom of the curve, \(\class{mjx-out}{4}\) at \(x=\class{mjx-in}{2}\).

The slope at one point is a number. The slopes at every point form a new function: the derivative.

The derivative of \(f\) is the function

\[\class{mjx-out}{f'(x)}=\lim_{\class{mjx-in}{h}\to0}\frac{f(x+\class{mjx-in}{h})-f(x)}{\class{mjx-in}{h}}\]

wherever this limit exists.

\(f'(x)\) is read “f prime of x”. So for \(f(x)=x^2\), \(f'(x)=2x\), and \(f'(1)=2\).

Writing \(y=f(x)\), another notation is \(\frac{dy}{dx}\), read “dee y by dee x”. It keeps the picture of rise over run, but it means the limit of that fraction, not a fraction.

The other \(\frac{0}{0}\) function on Limits was a slope too. With \(x\) in radians, \(\frac{\sin h}{h}\) is the slope of \(y=\sin x\) between \(0\) and \(h\). So the derivative of \(\sin x\) at \(0\) is \(1\).

Where There Is No Slope

A derivative is a limit, so it can fail in the same ways a limit can.

On Limits, a limit failed in three ways: a jump, a blow-up and a wobble. Each has a slope version, here at \(x=0\).

\(|x|\) has a corner. Its slope between \(0\) and \(h\) is \(-1\) for negative \(h\) and \(1\) for positive \(h\): a jump.

\(\sqrt[3]{x}\) stands vertical at \(0\). Its slope between \(0\) and \(h\) is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{h^2}}\), which grows without end: a blow-up. Its tangent is a vertical line, which has no slope.

\(x\sin\frac{1}{x}\), with its value at \(0\) set to \(0\), wobbles. Its slope between \(0\) and \(h\) is \(\sin\frac{1}{h}\), the wobble itself.

A function with a slope at a point is called differentiable there. All three are continuous at \(0\), with no hole and no jump, but none is differentiable.

And a function with a hole or a jump is never differentiable there. A hole leaves no point to measure from. Across a jump, the rise stays near the size of the jump while the run shrinks to nothing.

Rates of Change

Slope is a rate: how much the output changes per unit of input.

A dropped stone falls about \(5t^2\) metres in \(t\) seconds.

Between \(t=1\) and \(t=1+h\), its average speed is the distance fallen over the time taken:

\[\frac{5(1+h)^2-5}{h}=\frac{10h+5h^2}{h}=10+5h\]

As \(h\) closes in on \(0\), this closes in on \(10\). At one second, the stone is falling at about \(10\) metres per second.

This is the speed at one instant, the number a speedometer shows. Speed is the derivative of distance.

Repeating this at any time \(t\) gives a speed of \(10t\). Its slope, \(10\), is the stone's acceleration: its speed grows by about \(10\) metres per second, every second.

Exponentials

Inverse Operations: Radication and Logarithms

Reversing Multiplication

An inverse operation reverses the effect of another operation.

We start with:

\[4\]

We multiply it by \(3\):

\[4 \times 3 = \boxed{12}\]

But we can undo this by dividing by \(3\):

\[\frac{12}{3} = \boxed{4}\]

We could just as easily have started with:

\[3\]

And multiplied by \(4\):

\[3 \times 4 = \boxed{12}\]

And divided by \(4\):

\[\frac{12}{4} = \boxed{3}\]

Why Commutativity Matters

A number can be made by multiplying two factors.

One factor will denote the number of copies of the other factor we have.

The second factor will denote the value of each copy.

We could have \(a\) groups of \(b\) or \(b\) groups of \(a\).

Multiplication is commutative, meaning that changing the order of the factors does not change their product.

\[\boxed{a \times b = b \times a}\]

So…

Suppose we know:

  • Two factors multiply to produce \(12\).
  • One factor is \(3\).

There are two possible situations.

The first factor is unknown:

\[x \times 3 = 12\]

The second factor is unknown:

\[3 \times x = 12\]

But because multiplication is commutative:

\[3 \times x = x \times 3\]

Division gives us the answer to both situations:

\[x = \frac{12}{3} = \boxed{4}\]

Reversing Exponentiation

For multiplication:

\[a \times b = b \times a\]

The two inputs are interchangeable.

Therefore, whether the unknown is the first or second factor, we can recover it by dividing the product by the known factor (provided it is nonzero).

For exponentiation:

\[\class{mjx-base}{a}^{\class{mjx-exp}{b}} \neq \class{mjx-base}{b}^{\class{mjx-exp}{a}} \quad \text{in general}\]

The inputs are not interchangeable. Moreover, the base and exponent participate in fundamentally different ways, and recovering each requires a different mathematical procedure.

Consider:

\[\class{mjx-base}{2}^{\class{mjx-exp}{3}} = 8\]

Working backwards from the result of exponentiation has two different inverse operations, depending on whether we know the base or the exponent.

Knowing the exponent requires radication.

\[\class{mjx-base}{x}^{\class{mjx-exp}{3}} = 8 \qquad \sqrt[\class{mjx-exp}{3}]{8}=\class{mjx-base}{2}\]

Knowing the base requires logarithms.

\[\class{mjx-base}{2}^{\class{mjx-exp}{x}} = 8 \qquad \log_{\class{mjx-base}{2}}(8) =\class{mjx-exp}{3}\]

Radication: Recovering the Base

\[\class{mjx-base}{x}^{\class{mjx-exp}{3}} = 8\]

We know that we are taking three multiplicative steps to reach \(8\).

\[\class{mjx-base}{x} \cdot \class{mjx-base}{x} \cdot \class{mjx-base}{x} = 8\]

We do not know what the factors are in this product.

We can recover the factor by taking the cube root of \(8\):

\[\class{mjx-base}{x} = \sqrt[\class{mjx-exp}{3}]{8} = \boxed{\class{mjx-base}{2}}\]

More generally, for a positive base:

\[\boxed{\class{mjx-base}{a}^{\class{mjx-exp}{n}} = b \iff \class{mjx-base}{a} = \sqrt[\class{mjx-exp}{n}]{b}}\]

Logarithms: Recovering the Exponent

\[\class{mjx-base}{2}^{\class{mjx-exp}{x}} = 8\]

We know:

  • Our common ratio is multiplication by \(\class{mjx-base}{2}\).
  • We must reach \(8\).

We don't know:

  • How many multiplicative steps will this take?

We can recover it by “taking the logarithm of \(8\) to base \(\class{mjx-base}{2}\)”:

\[\class{mjx-exp}{x} = \log_{\class{mjx-base}{2}}(8) = \boxed{\class{mjx-exp}{3}}\]

More generally:

\[\boxed{\class{mjx-base}{a}^{\class{mjx-exp}{x}} = b \iff \class{mjx-exp}{x} = \log_{\class{mjx-base}{a}}(b)}\]

For real logarithms, \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), and \(b > 0\).

Why Power Adds Up: Orthogonal Frequencies

The Fourier transform splits a signal into sinusoidal waves and finds the amplitude of each (see Fourier Transform Visualisation). Amplitudes tell us which waves are there, but not how much of the whole signal each one accounts for. For that we need a measure of size that works for the whole signal, and whose parts add up to it. Which measure does that?

What Is a Signal’s Size?

The amplitude of a single oscillation is its maximum displacement from the centre line during that oscillation.

Since a sinusoidal wave is made of the same repeating oscillation, knowing the amplitude tells us about the strength of the wave as a whole. Every wave the Fourier transform finds is sinusoidal, so its amplitude describes it fully.

But the signal those waves add up to is not sinusoidal. For messy, complex signals, knowing the amplitude doesn’t tell us much about the wave as a whole, as it is not shared by the other fluctuations. It would only tell us the peak of the largest fluctuation, which can easily be an artefact, such as a blink.

Instead, we need a measure that uses the displacement of every point on the wave, not just the peaks and troughs: some kind of average of how far the points are from the centre line.

Two Ways to Average Size

How far is a point from the centre line on average?

The instantaneous displacement is the distance and direction (+ or −) from the centre line at one point on the wave.

We can’t simply average the instantaneous displacements. A sinusoidal wave is symmetrical about its centre line, so the positive and negative displacements cancel and the average is zero.

If we take the absolute value of each instantaneous displacement, the troughs flip into copies of the peaks.

On a sinusoidal wave, the average distance of a point from the centre line, the mean absolute displacement (MAD), is approximately 64% of the peak height.

Why 64% and not 50%?

Because the curves are all identical, finding the average vertical distance of a point on one is the same as finding it for the whole wave. So let’s just focus on one curve.

Curves like this spend more time at greater heights. Imagine cutting the curve at half its peak height, then compare how long it spends above and below that line. Slide to flatten each piece into a straight line whose length is the time it spans.

Why does a sinusoidal wave linger near its peaks and troughs? Remember that a sinusoidal wave is perfectly represented by plotting the height of a point moving steadily around a circle. Notice that the point’s height changes rapidly as it travels up or down the sides of the circle, and slowly as it travels along the top or bottom. See how these match the steep parts of the wave and its rounded peaks and troughs: the wave rushes through the centre line and lingers near its peaks and troughs.

Average Power

Squaring the instantaneous displacement at a single point gives the instantaneous power.

Squaring every instantaneous displacement first flips the troughs to peaks, just like taking the absolute value. Then every value is multiplied by itself. See if you can spot the significance in how this changes the shape of the wave.

So squaring all of the instantaneous displacements of a wave and taking their average gives the average power (P). For a sinusoidal wave, the average power is always half the squared amplitude: here, 16 ÷ 2 = 8 µV².

Why exactly half?

When we took the absolute values, the resulting wave had rounded tops and V-shaped bottoms, so it spent more time high up. When we square the wave, both the tops and the bottoms are rounded.

Squaring increases larger values more than smaller ones. A point that starts halfway up will only be a quarter of the way up after squaring, because the points above it have grown by an even greater ratio: 2 µV doubles to 4 µV², while the 4 µV peak quadruples to 16 µV². The lower a point starts, the further behind the peak it falls; we saw the same effect on the number line in Addition, Multiplication, Squaring. The overall effect is that we end up with as many points near the bottom as near the top.

In fact, the squared wave is itself a sinusoidal wave, swinging evenly between 0 and its peak. It spends as long in its top half as in its bottom half, so its average sits exactly halfway up.

For a sinusoidal wave, then, both measures follow directly from the amplitude: the MAD is about 64% of it, and the average power is half its square. The difference between them only shows when we combine waves.

The Test: Which One Adds Up?

We can add two sinusoidal waves together by adding their displacements at every point. The MAD of the combined wave does not equal the sum of the MADs of the constituent waves. However, the average power does. This only holds when the waves have different frequencies: two waves of the same frequency merge into one wave whose size depends on how they line up, so their powers don’t simply add.

This is the property we were looking for. Add waves of different frequencies together, and their average powers add up to the average power of the result. Their MADs don’t.

Why Power Adds Up

When combining waves, we could square them first and then add them together, or we could add them together and then square.

Adding together first

Squaring first

The two results look different: adding first makes taller spikes where the waves reinforce each other and deeper dips where they cancel. But their averages are the same.

Why? Take one moment where wave 1 is at 3 µV and wave 2 at 2 µV. Adding first, then squaring, gives one number; squaring first, then adding, gives another:

\[(3+2)^2 = 5^2 = 25\]
\[3^2+2^2 = 9+4 = 13\]

These give different results. But if we do this for every point on the waves and average, the two averages come out the same, as long as the waves have different frequencies.

The difference at each moment comes from the extra term in the expansion:

\[(a+b)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + \class{mjx-amp}{2ab}\]

Here the extra term is \(2 \times 3 \times 2 = 12\), which is exactly the gap between 25 and 13.

Sinusoidal Waves

The widget below plots the extra term \(2AB\) at every moment for two waves with an amplitude of 4 µV, at 2 Hz and 5 Hz. Each wave has a power of 8 µV², so their individual powers sum to 16 µV². Change the phase of wave B and watch the shaded areas.

Changing the phase reshapes the extra term but never its average. For sinusoidal waves of different frequencies, averaged over a stretch that holds whole cycles of both (or over a long enough stretch), the positive and negative extra terms cancel. So:

\[\class{mjx-pow}{\text{Power}(A+B)} = \class{mjx-pow}{\text{Power}(A)} + \class{mjx-pow}{\text{Power}(B)}\]

Switch wave B to 2 Hz to see the condition at work. With both waves at the same frequency, the extra term usually stays positive or negative on average, and the powers no longer add up.

Why should squaring be what makes this work? Think of walking 3 m east and then 4 m north: you end up 5 m from where you started. The distances don’t add up (3 + 4 ≠ 5), but their squares do (9 + 16 = 25). Rhythms at different frequencies behave like perpendicular directions in this sense, which is why it is their squares, their powers, that add. Mathematicians call signals like this orthogonal: multiplied together and averaged, they give zero. The fact that the powers of orthogonal components add up to the power of the whole is known as Parseval’s theorem.

What This Buys Us

Suppose the Fourier transform finds three waves in a signal: 2 µV at 2 Hz, 4 µV at 5 Hz and 6 µV at 10 Hz. Converting each amplitude to power, half its square, gives 2, 8 and 18 µV². Because the frequencies differ, these add up to 28 µV², the average power of the signal itself. Each frequency owns a definite share of it.

FrequencyAmplitudePowerShare
2 Hz2 µV2 µV²7%
5 Hz4 µV8 µV²29%
10 Hz6 µV18 µV²64%
Total28 µV²100%

The amplitudes add up to 12 µV, but that is not the size of anything in the signal: its peak depends on how the waves line up, and its MAD is different again. Amplitude tells us which waves are present; power tells us how much of the whole each one accounts for. That turns questions like “is there more alpha when the eyes are closed?” into questions with clear answers: how big is alpha’s share?

Real EEG is not a handful of clean waves. Its power is spread continuously across frequencies, so instead of a few bars we describe it as power per hertz: a power spectral density. Adding up the density over a range, such as 8–12 Hz for alpha, gives that band’s share of the total. This only works because power adds. An amplitude spectrum can show where the activity is, but adding it up over a band gives a share of nothing. Estimating the density from real, noisy recordings is the subject of PSD Estimation and Welch’s Method.

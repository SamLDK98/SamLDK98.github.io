Power Spectral Density

How is a signal’s power spread across frequencies?

From Amplitude to Power

An oscillation’s amplitude tells us how far it swings.

Its power is proportional to the amplitude squared.

\[\class{mjx-pow}{\text{power}} \propto \class{mjx-amp}{A}^2\]

Squaring does two things for us:

  • It removes the sign, so swings above and below zero count the same.
  • It makes power add up. Once the average voltage is removed, the power of all the frequencies together equals the variance of the signal.
\[\sum_{\text{all frequencies}} \class{mjx-pow}{\text{power}} = \text{variance of } x(t)\]

So a power spectrum is a breakdown of the signal’s variance by frequency.

For a sine wave of amplitude \(\class{mjx-amp}{A}\), the variance is exactly \(\frac{\class{mjx-amp}{A}^2}{2}\).

The Discrete Fourier Transform

An EEG recording is not a continuous curve. It is a list of \(N\) voltages sampled at a rate \(f_s\) (often \(250\) to \(1{,}000\) samples per second).

The discrete Fourier transform compares the recording with a set of oscillations, one at each frequency:

\[X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n \, e^{-i 2\pi k n / N}\]

Each \(X_k\) is a complex number. Its size tells us how strongly the recording matches the oscillation at frequency \(\class{mjx-freq}{f_k}\), and its angle tells us the phase.

\[\class{mjx-freq}{f_k} = \frac{k \, f_s}{N} \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, \frac{N}{2}\]

The frequencies stop at \(\frac{f_s}{2}\), the Nyquist frequency. Faster oscillations cannot be told apart from slower ones with fewer than two samples per cycle.

A power spectrum keeps the size of each \(X_k\) and throws away the angle.

The Periodogram

Squaring the size of each \(X_k\) and scaling it gives the periodogram:

\[\class{mjx-pow}{P(f_k)} = \frac{2}{f_s N} \, \lvert X_k \rvert^2\]

The \(2\) folds the negative frequencies, which mirror the positive ones for a real signal, onto the positive ones.

The units are µV²/Hz: power per hertz of frequency. This is why it is called a density.

Dividing by the width of each frequency bin means the values do not depend on how finely we chose to slice the frequencies. Summing the density over a range, times the bin width, gives the power in that range.

Frequency Resolution

The frequencies in the spectrum are spaced \(\frac{f_s}{N}\) apart. Since a recording of \(N\) samples lasts \(T = \frac{N}{f_s}\) seconds:

\[\boxed{\Delta \class{mjx-freq}{f} = \frac{1}{T}}\]

Only the length of the recording sets the frequency resolution. Sampling faster adds higher frequencies, but does not space the bins more finely.

The widget below shows two equal oscillations at \(10\) Hz and \(11\) Hz.

With half a second, the bins are \(2\) Hz apart, and the two oscillations blur into one peak.

With four seconds, the bins are \(0.25\) Hz apart, and the peaks separate cleanly.

Before transforming, each stretch of signal is usually tapered at its ends by a , which widens each peak a little.

The Fourier transform treats a stretch of recording as if it repeated forever.

If the signal’s value at the end does not match its value at the start, the repeat has a jump in it.

A jump needs many frequencies to describe it, so some power from each true peak spreads into its neighbours. This is spectral leakage.

A window fades the stretch in and out, so its ends meet at zero. The Hann window is the usual choice:

\[w_n = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 - \cos\frac{2\pi n}{N}\right)\]

Windowing trades a slightly wider peak for much less leakage far from the peak.

A Noisy Estimate

EEG is not a few clean oscillations. Much of it is irregular, and the periodogram of irregular activity is jagged.

Recording for longer does not smooth it. A longer recording gives finer frequency bins, but each bin is just as uncertain as before.

Welch’s method trades some resolution for a steadier estimate:

  1. Cut the recording into shorter segments, usually overlapping by half.
  2. Window each segment and compute its periodogram.
  3. Average the periodograms.

Here, \(64\) seconds of simulated EEG are cut into \(2\)-second segments, so each periodogram has \(0.5\) Hz bins.

One segment hides the structure. Averaging all \(63\) reveals it.

The segment length sets the trade-off. Longer segments give finer resolution but fewer segments to average.

Reading an EEG Spectrum

The averaged spectrum above is plotted on a logarithmic power axis, as EEG spectra usually are.

Notice that:

  • Power falls steadily as frequency rises, roughly in proportion to \(\frac{1}{f^{\chi}}\). This is the aperiodic background.
  • The alpha peak sits above that background rather than standing alone.

Without the log scale, the low frequencies would tower over everything and the alpha peak would be hard to see.

Band Power

To summarise a band, we add up the density across it:

\[\class{mjx-pow}{P_{\text{alpha}}} = \int_{8}^{13} \class{mjx-pow}{\text{PSD}}(\class{mjx-freq}{f}) \, d\class{mjx-freq}{f} \approx \sum_{8 \le f_k \le 13} \class{mjx-pow}{P(f_k)} \, \Delta \class{mjx-freq}{f}\]

This is the absolute band power, in µV².

Dividing by the power over a broad range (for example \(1\) to \(40\) Hz) gives the relative band power: the fraction of the signal’s variance in that band.

\[\text{relative alpha} = \frac{\class{mjx-pow}{P_{\text{alpha}}}}{\class{mjx-pow}{P_{1\text{–}40}}}\]

Relative power is less affected by things that scale the whole signal, such as skull thickness or electrode contact, but a change in one band also changes the relative power of all the others.

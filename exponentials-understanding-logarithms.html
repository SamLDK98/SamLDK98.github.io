Exponentials

Understanding Logarithms

Logarithms Count the Multiplicative Steps Along the Number Line

Imagine a number on the number line:

\[64\]

Division counts the number of additive steps needed to walk from \(0\) to \(64\).

\[\frac{64}{2}=32 \ \text{steps}\]

Logarithms count the number of multiplicative steps needed to walk from \(1\) to \(64\).

\[\log_{\class{mjx-base}{2}}(64)=\class{mjx-exp}{6} \ \text{steps}\]

While additive steps are of equal distance, multiplicative steps are of equal ratio.

When you look at a power, you can think of each element like this:

  • Base → the multiplicative factor.
  • Exponent → the number of equal multiplicative steps.
  • Output → the destination.

When we compute a logarithm, we are finding the numerical value of the exponent.

Just as Adding Exponents Corresponds to Multiplying Outputs, So Too Does Adding Logarithms

The product of powers rule shows that multiplication in the world of powers corresponds to addition in the world of exponents.

\[\boxed{\class{mjx-base}{b}^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}} \cdot \class{mjx-base}{b}^{\class{mjx-exp}{n}} = \class{mjx-base}{b}^{\class{mjx-exp}{m+n}}}\]

We can express the same thing in terms of logarithms:

\[b^{\log_{b}(x)} \cdot b^{\log_{b}(y)} = b^{\log_{b}(x)+\log_{b}(y)}\]

Where the logarithms come from

Since:

\[\class{mjx-exp}{m} = \log_{b}(x), \qquad \class{mjx-exp}{n} =\log_{b}(y)\]

\(x\) and \(y\) are just the unknown outputs of \(b^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}}\) and \(b^{\class{mjx-exp}{n}}\):

\[b^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}} = x \qquad \text{and} \qquad b^{\class{mjx-exp}{n}} = y\]

Further, we can express this relationship between multiplication and addition without the relationship only existing within the exponents.

Let's rearrange our equation.

The Left Hand Side

\[b^{\log_{b}(x)} \cdot b^{\log_{b}(y)}\]

Recall the first identity showing that exponentiation undoes logarithms:

\[\boxed{a^{\log_{a}(b)}=b}\]

The logarithm \(\log_{a}(b)\) gives the value of the exponent required to raise \(a\) to equal \(b\).

Since this value is what \(a\) is being raised to, the term must equal \(b\)!

We can apply this logic to each of the factors on the left hand side here.

\[b^{\log_{b}(x)} = x \qquad b^{\log_{b}(y)} = y\]

The LHS therefore simplifies to:

\[xy\]

The Right Hand Side

\[b^{\log_{b}(x)+\log_{b}(y)}\]

We want to remove exponents from our equation.

Logarithms undo exponentiation, as we saw in the second identity:

\[\boxed{\log_{a}(a^x)=x}\]

The base here is \(b\), so we must take the logarithm to the base \(b\). Then the exponent of the input, which is the expression \(\log_{b}(x) + \log_{b}(y)\), becomes the output:

\[\log_{b}\!\left(b^{\log_{b}(x)+\log_{b}(y)}\right) = \log_{b}(x)+\log_{b}(y)\]

Both Sides

Since we applied a logarithm to one side, we have to apply it to the other:

\[\boxed{\log_b(xy) = \log_b(x) + \log_b(y)}\]

This is called the product rule for logarithms.

It applies for \(x, y > 0\), where \(b > 0\) and \(b \neq 1\).

Why Bother with Logarithms?

If we know exponents can convert multiplication problems into addition, why bother with expressing them in terms of logarithms like this?

The advantage is that logarithms allow us to start with any positive numbers, even when their exponents are unknown.

For example, we know that \(17 \times 43 = 731\), but neither \(17\) nor \(43\) is an obvious integer power of \(10\).

Logarithms allow us to find the exponents that represent these numbers in a common base:

\[\log_{10}(17)+\log_{10}(43)=\log_{10}(731)\]

We can therefore work with the exponents directly, without first having to express the original numbers as powers.

This is the significance of logarithms: they provide a general correspondence between multiplication of positive numbers and addition of real numbers.

The product of powers rule establishes this relationship; logarithms allow us to use it starting from the numbers themselves.

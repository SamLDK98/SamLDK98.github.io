Exponentials

Understanding Logarithms

Logarithms Count the Multiplicative Steps Along the Number Line

Imagine a number on the number line:

\[64\]

Division counts the number of additive steps needed to walk from \(0\) to \(64\).

\[\frac{64}{2}=32 \ \text{steps}\]

Logarithms count the number of multiplicative steps needed to walk from \(1\) to \(64\).

\[\log_{\class{mjx-base}{2}}(64)=\class{mjx-exp}{6} \ \text{steps}\]

While additive steps are of equal distance, multiplicative steps are of equal ratio.

When you look at a power, you can think of each element like this:

  • Base → the multiplicative factor.
  • Exponent → the number of equal multiplicative steps.
  • Output → the destination.

When we compute a logarithm, we are finding the numerical value of the exponent.

Just as Adding Exponents Corresponds to Multiplying Outputs, So Too Does Adding Logarithms

The product of powers rule shows that multiplication in the world of powers corresponds to addition in the world of exponents.

\[\boxed{\class{mjx-base}{b}^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}} \cdot \class{mjx-base}{b}^{\class{mjx-exp}{n}} = \class{mjx-base}{b}^{\class{mjx-exp}{m+n}}}\]

We can express the same thing in terms of logarithms:

\[b^{\log_{b}(x)} \cdot b^{\log_{b}(y)} = b^{\log_{b}(x)+\log_{b}(y)}\]

Where the logarithms come from

Since:

\[\class{mjx-exp}{m} = \log_{b}(x), \qquad \class{mjx-exp}{n} =\log_{b}(y)\]

\(x\) and \(y\) are just the unknown outputs of \(b^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}}\) and \(b^{\class{mjx-exp}{n}}\):

\[b^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}} = x \qquad \text{and} \qquad b^{\class{mjx-exp}{n}} = y\]

Further, we can express this relationship between multiplication and addition without the relationship only existing within the exponents.

Let's rearrange our equation.

The Left Hand Side

\[b^{\log_{b}(x)} \cdot b^{\log_{b}(y)}\]

Recall the first identity showing that exponentiation undoes logarithms:

\[\boxed{a^{\log_{a}(b)}=b}\]

The logarithm \(\log_{a}(b)\) gives the value of the exponent required to raise \(a\) to equal \(b\).

Since this value is what \(a\) is being raised to, the term must equal \(b\)!

We can apply this logic to each of the factors on the left hand side here.

\[b^{\log_{b}(x)} = x \qquad b^{\log_{b}(y)} = y\]

The LHS therefore simplifies to:

\[xy\]

The Right Hand Side

\[b^{\log_{b}(x)+\log_{b}(y)}\]

We want to remove exponents from our equation.

Logarithms undo exponentiation, as we saw in the second identity:

\[\boxed{\log_{a}(a^x)=x}\]

The base here is \(b\), so we must take the logarithm to the base \(b\). Then the exponent of the input, which is the expression \(\log_{b}(x) + \log_{b}(y)\), becomes the output:

\[\log_{b}\!\left(b^{\log_{b}(x)+\log_{b}(y)}\right) = \log_{b}(x)+\log_{b}(y)\]

Both Sides

Since we applied a logarithm to one side, we have to apply it to the other:

\[\boxed{\log_b(xy) = \log_b(x) + \log_b(y)}\]

This is called the product rule for logarithms.

It applies for \(x, y > 0\), where \(b > 0\) and \(b \neq 1\).

Why Bother with Logarithms?

If we know exponents can convert multiplication problems into addition, why bother with expressing them in terms of logarithms like this?

The advantage is that logarithms allow us to start with any positive numbers, even when their exponents are unknown.

For example, we know that \(17 \times 43 = 731\), but neither \(17\) nor \(43\) is an obvious integer power of \(10\).

Logarithms allow us to find the exponents that represent these numbers in a common base:

\[\log_{10}(17)+\log_{10}(43)=\log_{10}(731)\]

We can therefore work with the exponents directly, without first having to express the original numbers as powers.

This is the significance of logarithms: they provide a general correspondence between multiplication of positive numbers and addition of real numbers.

The product of powers rule establishes this relationship; logarithms allow us to use it starting from the numbers themselves.

Bonus: Why Convert the Product of Powers Rule at All?

The product rule for logarithms does not introduce a new relationship. It takes one we already had and makes it usable in the other direction.

Removing the exponent notation is not, by itself, what makes logarithms useful. The real advantage is that logarithms give us a systematic way to turn a multiplication of any positive numbers into an addition.

What the Exponent Rule Already Tells Us

Consider:

\[\class{mjx-base}{2}^{\class{mjx-exp}{3}}\cdot\class{mjx-base}{2}^{\class{mjx-exp}{4}}=\class{mjx-base}{2}^{\class{mjx-exp}{3+4}}=\class{mjx-base}{2}^{\class{mjx-exp}{7}}\]

Multiplication becomes addition here because both numbers are already written as powers of the same base.

But consider a different problem:

\[17\times43\]

How would we use the product of powers rule here?

We would first need to find exponents \(\class{mjx-exp}{m}\) and \(\class{mjx-exp}{n}\) such that:

\[\class{mjx-base}{10}^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}}=17,\qquad\class{mjx-base}{10}^{\class{mjx-exp}{n}}=43\]

The exponent rule then tells us that:

\[17\times43=\class{mjx-base}{10}^{\class{mjx-exp}{m+n}}\]

But the exponent rule does not tell us how to find \(\class{mjx-exp}{m}\) and \(\class{mjx-exp}{n}\).

That is the missing capability. Try finding them by sliding the exponent, then let the logarithm find them for you.

Logarithms Provide the Missing Capability

A logarithm is precisely the operation that finds those exponents.

\[\class{mjx-exp}{m}=\log_{\class{mjx-base}{10}}(17)\approx1.23045\]
\[\class{mjx-exp}{n}=\log_{\class{mjx-base}{10}}(43)\approx1.63347\]

We can now add them:

\[\class{mjx-exp}{m+n}\approx2.86392\]

And recover the product:

\[\class{mjx-base}{10}^{\class{mjx-exp}{2.86392}}\approx731\]

So we have calculated \(17\times43=731\) using logarithms and addition.

Notice the difference:

  • The exponent rule establishes that adding exponents corresponds to multiplying powers.
  • The logarithm finds the relevant exponents for numbers we already have.

The second capability is what makes the first useful as a general method of calculation.

Why This Mattered Historically

Before electronic calculators, people used tables of logarithms to carry out difficult multiplications.

Instead of a lengthy multiplication of two many-digit numbers, you could:

  1. Look up the logarithm of each number in a table.
  2. Add the logarithms.
  3. Look up the number whose logarithm is that sum (the antilogarithm).

The difficult multiplication was replaced by one addition and three table lookups.

A table only needs to list the logarithms of numbers from \(1\) to \(10\). Every other positive number is one of these multiplied by a power of \(10\), and by the product rule, multiplying by \(10^{c}\) just adds \(c\) to the logarithm: \(\log_{10}(17) = 1 + \log_{10}(1.7)\).

Of course, calculating logarithms from scratch would often be more work than multiplying directly. Their computational advantage depended on having the logarithms already worked out and tabulated.

A Deeper Significance

The computational use is only one consequence of a more general property.

Consider how multiplication behaves:

\[2\times3\times5\times7=210\]

Now consider their logarithms:

\[\log_b(2)+\log_b(3)+\log_b(5)+\log_b(7)=\log_b(210)\]

A multiplicative relationship between positive numbers has become an additive relationship between real numbers.

This works whether the numbers are whole numbers, fractions, irrational numbers or any other positive real numbers. A factor less than \(1\) has a negative logarithm, so multiplying by it steps backwards.

This is not just a convenient computational trick. The logarithm preserves the structure of multiplication while representing it through addition.

In abstract algebra, this is an example of an isomorphism: a reversible correspondence between two mathematical systems that preserves their operations.

Three Successive Discoveries

First: the product of powers rule.

\[\class{mjx-base}{b}^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}}\class{mjx-base}{b}^{\class{mjx-exp}{n}}=\class{mjx-base}{b}^{\class{mjx-exp}{m+n}}\]

Multiplying powers with a common base corresponds to adding their exponents.

Second: the logarithm.

\[\class{mjx-exp}{m}=\log_{\class{mjx-base}{b}}(x)\]

Given any positive number and a valid base, we can find the exponent that represents that number as a power of the base.

Third: the product rule for logarithms.

\[\boxed{\log_b(xy)=\log_b(x)+\log_b(y)}\]

We can now describe multiplication between any positive numbers directly as addition between their logarithms.

The third result follows from the first two. It is not a new, independent principle.

The deeper insight is that the multiplicative structure of the positive real numbers can be represented exactly by the additive structure of the real numbers. Logarithms are the function that establishes this correspondence.

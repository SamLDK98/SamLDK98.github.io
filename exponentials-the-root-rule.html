Exponentials

The Root Rule

Fractional Exponents

What does \(a^{\frac{1}{2}}\) even mean?

So far:

  • Increasing the exponent by \(1\) means we multiply the previous power's value by the base.
  • Decreasing the exponent by \(1\) means we divide the previous power's value by the base.

Let's say we have:

\[5^{\frac{1}{2}}\]

If \(5^0=1\) and \(5^1=5\)...

To go from an exponent of \(0\) to \(1\), we multiplied the previous power's value (\(1\)) by the base (\(5\)).

Perhaps we could multiply the previous power's value by half the base?

We know that increasing the exponent by \(1\) results in multiplying the previous power's value by the base.

So perhaps if we only increase the exponent halfway, we should be multiplying the previous power's value by only half as strong a factor?

By this logic we would multiply \(1\) by \(2.5\).

Why Not 2.5?

However, a key thing to realise about exponentiation is why \(5^{\frac{1}{2}}\) does not equal \(2.5\), but instead equals \(\sqrt{5}\).

The mistake in thinking \(5^{\frac{1}{2}}=2.5\) comes from treating exponents as if they were about how much to multiply by, rather than how many times.

If the exponent \(3\) means we are creating three copies of the base, then it would make sense to think that the exponent \(3.5\) means we should take three and a half copies of the base and multiply them together.

\(5^{3.5}\):

\[5\cdot5\cdot5\cdot2.5\]

But this would be treating exponents as linear, like addition.

In fact, exponentiation is multiplicative. The steps are not spaced evenly: they are scaled.

So the difference between \(5^0\) and \(5^{\frac{1}{2}}\) is not the same as the difference between \(5^{\frac{1}{2}}\) and \(5^1\). What stays the same is the factor: each half step multiplies by the same number.

The Square Root

So why does \(5^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{5}\)?

Because whatever the value of the halfway point is, multiplying by an equal factor again must give the base.

Therefore we need a number that multiplied by itself would equal the base.

So this must be true:

\[a^{\frac{1}{2}}\times a^{\frac{1}{2}}=a^1=a\]

Therefore, \(a^{\frac{1}{2}}\) isn’t “half the base,” it’s the number that, when multiplied by itself, gives the base — i.e. the square root of the base.

\[\boxed{a^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{a}}\]

Other Fractions

The same argument works for other fractions.

Three thirds make a whole, so three equal factors of \(a^{\frac{1}{3}}\) must give the base:

\[a^{\frac{1}{3}}\times a^{\frac{1}{3}}\times a^{\frac{1}{3}}=a^1=a\]

\(a^{\frac{1}{3}}\) is the number that gives the base when three copies of it are multiplied together: the cube root, \(\sqrt[3]{a}\). For example, \(8^{\frac{1}{3}}=2\), since \(2\cdot2\cdot2=8\).

In the same way, \(n\) equal steps of \(\frac{1}{n}\) make one whole step, so \(n\) equal factors of \(a^{\frac{1}{n}}\) must give the base. This is the root rule:

\[\boxed{a^{\frac{1}{n}}=\sqrt[n]{a}}\]

Other fractions are made of these steps. \(a^{\frac{2}{3}}\) is two steps of a third:

\[a^{\frac{2}{3}}=a^{\frac{1}{3}}\times a^{\frac{1}{3}}=\left(\sqrt[3]{a}\right)^2\]

For example, \(8^{\frac{2}{3}}=2\cdot2=4\). In general, \(a^{\frac{m}{n}}=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m\).

A negative fraction steps down instead, dividing as with negative integer exponents: \(a^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\).

For fractional exponents we take the base to be positive. No real number multiplied by itself gives \(-4\), so \((-4)^{\frac{1}{2}}\) has no real value.

A note on the historical development of exponents

The level of abstraction we are now at is far removed from geometric intuition. It was a difficult progression for mathematicians to work towards historically.

\(a^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a}\) belongs to the Newton–Euler era. John Wallis showed how useful negative and fractional exponents could be in the 1650s, and Isaac Newton used them in his binomial series of 1676.

From then on, exponents weren’t just “times you multiply”: they became part of a continuous number system that could describe roots, fractions, and even irrationals. In 1748 Euler placed \(a^{\sqrt{7}}\) between \(a^2\) and \(a^3\).

By the 18th century, through the work of mathematicians such as Euler (1707–1783), exponents had been fully unified:

  • \(a^0=1\)
  • \(a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}\)
  • \(a^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a}\)

Euler’s Elements of Algebra (1770) reaches \(a^0=1\) just as the first page does: dividing by \(a\) lowers the exponent by \(1\).

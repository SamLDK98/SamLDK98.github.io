Calculus

Derivatives

The derivative is a measure of the instantaneous rate of change of a function at a particular point.

It tells us how sensitive the output is to a change in the input at that point.

Geometrically, it is the slope of the tangent line at that point.

Differentiation is the process of finding the derivative.

Slope

The change in output for a change in input.

\[\Large \frac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}}\]
\[\boxed{m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}}\]

Geometrically, we move the second point of a secant line closer and closer to an original point until the distance between them approaches zero.

Algebraically, we evaluate the function at \(x\) and \(x +h\), then simplify the difference quotient formula:

\[\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\]

The subsequent simplification is an expression of the secant slope between two points.

For example:

\[\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}= 2x + h\]

The expression \(2x + h\) represents the secant slope. Its limit is \(2x\), which is the derivative.

For the functions we will initially work with, setting \(h=0\) in the simplified difference quotient will typically give the derivative; however, things will get more complicated later.

For the functions we will initially work with, we can often simplify the difference quotient into an expression that is defined at \(h=0\). Substituting \(h=0\) into that simplified expression then gives us the limit, and therefore the derivative.

We can present this using the limit notation:

\[f'(x)=\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\]

The derivative of a straight line

Imagine:

\[f(x)= 2x + 3\]

We evaluate two points:

\[f(1)= 2+3 = 5\]
\[f(2)=2(2)+3=7\]

And find their slope:

\[\large \frac{7-5}{2-1} = \frac{2}{1}= \boxed{m=2}\]
\[\boxed{f'(x)=2}\]

Linear functions have a constant rate of change. The slope between any two points tells us the derivative of the function at any point.

The derivative of a quadratic function

\[f(x)=x^2\]

The rate of change now varies.

To know the slope at \(x=4\) we could select a second point on the curve, connect a secant line, and find its slope to give an approximation.

We could then move the second point closer to \((4,16)\) and take an even better approximation.

As we move the second point closer to \((4, 16)\), the secant slopes appear to approach 8.

But how do we know that they approach exactly 8? Could they continue decreasing towards 7.95, or some other value?

We need something more reliable than observing a numerical pattern.

Deriving a General Formula for the Secant Slope (the difference quotient)

Our inputs:

  1. \(x\)
  2. \(x+h\)

Our outputs:

  1. \(f(x)\)
  2. \(f(x+h)\)

We use \(h\) as it references ‘horizontal displacement’.

  • If \(h >0\): The second point lies to the right of the first
  • If \(h <0\): The second point lies to the left.

Slope formula:

\[\Large \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\]

Our change in inputs (\(\Delta x\)) is just \(h\):

\[(x+h)-x= \boxed{h}\]

Our change in outputs (\(\Delta y\)):

\[\boxed{f(x+h)-f(x)}\]

The general slope formula for the secant slope is therefore:

\[\boxed{\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}\]

Deriving a General Formula for the Secant Slope for a Particular Function

\[f(x)=x^2\]

Evaluate the function algebraically at \(f(x)\) and \(f(x+h)\):

  1. \(x^2\)
  2. \((x+h)^2 \to x^2 +2hx+h^2\)

General formula:

\[\boxed{\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}\]

Becomes:

\[\frac{x^2 +2hx+h^2-x^2}{h}\]
\[\frac{2hx+h^2}{h}\]

We can cancel the \(h\).

\[\boxed{2x + h}\]

This is the general formula for a secant slope between two points on the function \(f(x)=x^2\).

We are curious about the slope at \(x=4\).

We know the secant line between \(x=4\) and some other point must be:

\[2(4)+h= 8+h\]

If \(h\) is positive:

  • As we increase \(h\), the second point moves further away.
  • As we decrease \(h\), the second point moves closer and closer.

We can literally move it as close as we like. We want to get something really close to 0, but not 0 itself, say, 0.00000001.

\[m= 8.00000001\]

Notice that we can't go smaller than \(8\) itself for a positive \(h\).

We call \(8\) the limit of the secant slopes of the function at this point.

  • It is the lower bound for secant slopes approaching from the right.
  • It is the upper bound for secant slopes approaching from the left.

This limiting value is the derivative of \(f(x) = x^2\) at \(x=4\):

\[\boxed{f'(4)=8}\]

The Limit Definition of the Derivative

We can express the entire process of finding a derivative using a single mathematical formula:

\[f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\]

This is called the limit definition of the derivative, or differentiation from first principles.

NotationMeaning
\(f'(x)\)The derivative of the function \(f\) at input \(x\).
\(\lim\)The limit: the value an expression approaches.
\(h\to0\)The horizontal separation between our two points approaches zero.
\(f(x)\)The output at our first point.
\(f(x+h)\)The output at our second point.
\(f(x+h)-f(x)\)The difference between the outputs, \(\Delta y\).
\(h\)The difference between the inputs, \(\Delta x\).

The expression inside the limit is our familiar difference quotient, which calculates the secant slope between two points.

The limit tells us to investigate what this slope approaches as the separation between those points approaches zero.

Provided this limit exists as a finite number, its value is the derivative.

Applying the notation

For our function:

\[f(x)=x^2\]

We write:

\[f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}\]

Simplifying the difference quotient:

\[f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}(2x+h)\]

As \(h\) approaches zero, the expression approaches \(2x\).

Therefore:

\[\boxed{f'(x)=2x}\]

If we want the derivative at a particular input, such as \(x=4\), we evaluate this derivative function:

\[f'(4)=2(4)=\boxed{8}\]

We have now expressed our original procedure in a general mathematical form that can be applied to other functions.

This was all rather untroublesome thanks to how easy it was to simplify the difference quotient for \(f(x)=x^2\). However, the difficulty increases as when our functions result in more complicated algebraic expressions in the numerator of the difference quotient.

Exponentials

Moving Between Exponents

What happens to the value of a power as we move continuously between two exponents?

Increasing a Coefficient by a Fraction

Increasing a coefficient by a whole number adds that many copies of \(x\).

\[3x = x + x + x\]
\[(\class{mjx-value}{1}+3)x = x + x + x + \class{mjx-value}{x}\]

Increasing a coefficient by a fraction adds that much of \(x\).

\[(\class{mjx-value}{0.25}+3)x = x + x + x +\ \class{mjx-value}{?}\]

\(\frac{1}{4}\) of an addition is the value that when added four times gives \(x\):

\[q + q + q + q = x\]
\[4q = x\]

Division by four is the operation that finds the value of one part if four equal additive parts must reach some value.

\[\frac{x}{4} = q\]

So increasing the coefficient by a quarter looks like:

\[(0.25+3)x = x + x + x + \frac{x}{4}\]

Increasing an Exponent by a Fraction

Increasing an exponent by a whole number adds another factor of \(x\) to the product.

\[x^{3} = x \cdot x \cdot x\]
\[x^{3+\class{mjx-value}{1}} = x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot \class{mjx-value}{x}\]

If increasing by a fraction added that much of \(x\) as a factor to the product, we might get something like:

\[x^{3+0.5} = x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot \frac{x}{2}\]

Imagine \(x=10\):

\[10^{3+0.5} = 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 5\]

Now imagine we added another \(\frac{1}{2}\) to the exponent.

\[10^{3+0.5+0.5} = 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 5 \cdot 5\]

Yikes! Now we have ended up multiplying by \(25\) while only increasing the exponent by \(1\):

\[10^{3+1} = 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 25\]

We forgot to appreciate that our exponents correspond to multiplicative growth.

Increasing an exponent by a half does not take us halfway to the value of \(x\); it takes us halfway along the multiplicative journey to \(x\).

We therefore want to multiply by the factor that takes us halfway along this multiplicative journey.

\[a \cdot a = 10\]
\[a^2 = 10\]
\[a = \sqrt{10}\]

Therefore:

\[10^{3+0.5} = 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot \sqrt{10}\]

Or let's say we are adding \(\frac{1}{4}\) to an exponent.

One quarter of a multiplication of \(x\) is the value that, when multiplied by itself four times, gives \(x\).

\[a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a = x\]
\[a^4 = x\]

Radication (or taking roots) is the operation that finds the value of the multiplicative factor for a given output and number of equal multiplicative parts.

The number of multiplicative parts (or steps) is denoted by the index above the radical:

\[a = \sqrt[4]{x}\]

So increasing the exponent by a quarter looks like:

\[x^{3+0.25} = x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot \sqrt[4]{x}\]

Moving Between Exponents

Let's return to the overarching question.

What happens to the value of a power as we move continuously between two exponents?

\[8^1 = 8 \qquad 8^2 = 64\]

We could break the multiplicative journey from \(8\) to \(64\) down into any number of steps.

Let's choose \(10\) steps.

Our exponent increases by \(+1\) in additive steps, so say \(x\) is one step:

\[10x = 1\]
\[x = 0.1\]

Our value must increase by \(\frac{64}{8} = 8\) in multiplicative steps, so say \(r\) is one step:

\[r^{10} = 8\]
\[r = \sqrt[10]{8}\]
\[r \approx 1.23114\]

The amount that one step changes the value becomes increasingly greater.

Increasing the exponent by \(+0.1\) increases the power's value by \(\times 1.23114\).

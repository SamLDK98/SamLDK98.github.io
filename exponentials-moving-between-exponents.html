Exponentials

Moving Between Exponents

What happens to the value of a power as we move continuously between two exponents?

Previously we saw that increasing a coefficient produces additive changes in the output, while increasing an exponent produces multiplicative changes in the output.

Let's take a closer look at this.

Increasing a Coefficient by a Fraction

Think of coefficients in terms of repeated addition:

\[3x = x + x + x\]

Increasing by a whole number adds that many copies of \(x\):

\[(1+3)x = x + x + x + x\]

Increasing a coefficient by a fraction adds that much of \(x\).

One quarter of an addition of \(x\) is the value that, added four times, gives \(x\).

\[a + a + a + a = x\]

Dividing by four is the operation that finds the value of one part if four equal additive parts must reach some value.

\[\frac{x}{4} = a\]

So increasing the coefficient by a quarter looks like:

\[(0.25+3)x = x + x + x + \frac{x}{4}\]

Increasing an Exponent by a Fraction

Think of exponents in terms of repeated multiplication:

\[x^{3} = x \cdot x \cdot x\]

Increasing by a whole number adds another factor of \(x\) to the product:

\[x^{3+1} = x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x\]

If increasing by a fraction added that much of \(x\) as a factor to the product, we would get something like this:

\[x^{3+0.5} = x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot \frac{x}{2}\]

Imagine \(x=10\):

\[10^{3+0.5} = 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 5\]

Now imagine we added another \(\frac{1}{2}\) to the exponent.

\[10^{3+0.5+0.5} = 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 5 \cdot 5\]

Yikes! Now we have ended up multiplying by \(25\) while only increasing the exponent by \(1\):

\[10^{3+1} = 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 25 \;\neq\; 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10\]

We forgot to appreciate that our exponents correspond to multiplicative growth.

Increasing an exponent by a half does not take us halfway to the value of \(x\); it takes us halfway along the multiplicative journey to \(x\).

We therefore want to multiply by the factor that takes us halfway along this multiplicative journey.

\[a \cdot a = 10\]
\[a^2 = 10\]
\[a = \sqrt{10}\]

Therefore:

\[10^{3+0.5} = 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot \sqrt{10}\]

Fractions can represent parts of an additive journey or a multiplicative journey.

Let's say we are adding \(\frac{1}{4}\) to an exponent.

One quarter of a multiplication by \(x\) is the value that, when multiplied by itself four times, gives \(x\).

\[a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a = x\]
\[a^4 = x\]

Radication (or taking roots) is the operation that finds the value of the multiplicative factor for a given output and number of equal multiplicative parts.

The number of multiplicative parts (or steps) is denoted by the index above the radical:

\[a = \sqrt[4]{x}\]

So increasing the exponent by a quarter looks like:

\[x^{3+0.25} = x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot \sqrt[4]{x}\]

A Full Journey Between Two Whole Numbers

Let's now consider a full journey between two whole-number coefficients and exponents.

We will break down the additive and multiplicative journeys into ten parts.

As a Coefficient Increases by 1

Imagine:

\[x = 8\]

From:

\[x \to 2x\]

Is:

\[8 \to 16\]

But how does the change from \(8\) to \(16\) occur along the journey?

Take a look:

Term\(x=8\)Change
\(1x\)8–
\(1.1x\)8.8+0.8
\(1.2x\)9.6+0.8
\(1.3x\)10.4+0.8
\(1.4x\)11.2+0.8

\(1.6x\)12.8+0.8
\(1.7x\)13.6+0.8
\(1.8x\)14.4+0.8
\(1.9x\)15.2+0.8

The change occurs at a constant additive rate: increasing the coefficient by \(0.1\) \((\frac{1}{10})\) increases the output by \(+0.8\), always.

An increase from \(1.2x \to 1.4x\) increases the output by the same amount that an increase from \(1.7x \to 1.9x\) does: \(+1.6\).

As an Exponent Increases by 1

Imagine:

\[x = 8\]

From:

\[x^{1} \to x^{2}\]

Is:

\[8 \to 64\]

But how does the change from \(8\) to \(64\) occur along the journey?

Take a look:

The change occurs at a constant multiplicative rate.

Increasing the exponent by \(\frac{1}{10}\) increases the output by a factor that can be multiplied by itself \(10\) times to give \(x=8\).

That is:

\[\sqrt[10]{8} \approx 1.2311\]
  • If we increase the exponent by \(0.1\), we increase the current output by approximately \(\times 1.2311\).

Consider the difference between \(x^{1.2} \to x^{1.4}\):

\[18.379 - 12.126 = \boxed{6.253}\]

While the difference between \(x^{1.7}\) and \(x^{1.9}\):

\[51.984 - 34.297 = \boxed{17.687}\]

However, the ratio across both increments is identical:

\[\frac{18.379}{12.126} \approx 1.5157\]
\[\frac{51.984}{34.297} \approx 1.5157\]

Notice that the exponent increased by \(0.2\) in this example, hence the greater ratio: \(1.2311 \times 1.2311 \approx 1.5157\).

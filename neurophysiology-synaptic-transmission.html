Synaptic Transmission

An action potential cannot jump the gap between two neurons. It has to be converted into a chemical, thrown across, and converted back. Here is that machinery, in three dimensions, with the controls in your hands.

The gapWhere the wire runs out

Chapter 01 ended with a spike travelling down an axon, regenerating itself indefinitely. That works right up until the axon stops. Between one neuron and the next there is a gap of about twenty nanometres, and no electrical continuity across it at all.

So the signal changes medium. The arriving spike triggers the release of a chemical, the chemical drifts across the gap, and on the far side it opens channels that start a new electrical event. It is a deliberate bottleneck — slower and noisier than a wire — and it is where almost everything interesting about nervous systems happens, because a junction that has to be actively maintained is a junction that can be adjusted.

The chainSix steps, each gating the next

Every link below is a place where the signal can be amplified, weakened, or stopped outright. The widget lights each one up as it happens.

  1. The action potential arrives at the terminal and depolarises it.
  2. Calcium channels open. These are voltage-gated, like the sodium channels of chapter 01, but they let Ca²⁺ in — and Ca²⁺ is a signal, not just a charge.
  3. Vesicles fuse. Calcium binds the machinery holding transmitter-filled vesicles against the membrane, and they empty into the gap. This step is steeply calcium-dependent, which is why it is the main volume knob.
  4. Transmitter crosses the cleft by plain diffusion. Twenty nanometres takes microseconds; this is not the slow part.
  5. Receptors bind and open. Transmitter docks into receptors on the far membrane, which open a pore.
  6. Ions flow and the postsynaptic membrane potential moves. Whether that moves it toward firing or away is set entirely by which ion the pore admits.

The physicsOne extra current

The postsynaptic cell in the widget is the same Hodgkin–Huxley membrane from chapter 01 — same equations, same parameters, genuinely the same code. The synapse adds exactly one term to the current balance:

Isyn = gsyn(t)  ·  (V − Esyn)

a conductance that opens when transmitter binds, times the distance from that channel's own equilibrium potential

That is the whole of it. gsyn rises when receptors are occupied and falls as transmitter unbinds and is cleared. The two-stage chain behind it is just as plain — transmitter in the cleft decays away, and bound receptors track it:

dCdt = −kclear C      dBdt = kon C (1 − B) − koff B

C = transmitter in the cleft, B = fraction of receptors bound, g_syn = ḡ_syn · B

The one thing that decides excitation from inhibition is Esyn. Glutamate receptors pass sodium and potassium together, giving Esyn ≈ 0 mV — far above rest, so current flows inward and the cell depolarises. GABA receptors pass chloride, giving Esyn ≈ −75 mV, just below rest. Same equation, opposite sign, entirely because of which ion fits through the hole.

QuantaWhy the calcium knob is the interesting one

Vesicles are the reason transmitter comes in lumps. Each one holds roughly a fixed amount, so the terminal cannot release a smooth continuum — it releases one packet, or four, or none. Turn the calcium slider and you are setting how many fuse.

What comes out is strikingly linear, right up until it isn't. One vesicle produces a 1.0 mV bump. Two produce 2.0, three produce 3.2, four produce 4.7. Five produce a hundred-millivolt action potential. The synapse adds up its inputs in a perfectly graded way, and then hands the result to a membrane that only has two answers.

ExperimentsFour things worth doing

01 — threshold Find the fifth vesicle

Set calcium to 1 and fire. A 1 mV blip. Step up one vesicle at a time, firing at each: 2.0, 3.2, 4.7 mV — all just bumps. At five, the same small increment produces a full spike. Nothing about the synapse changed; you crossed the membrane's threshold.

02 — inhibition Watch GABA do almost nothing

Switch to GABA and fire hard. The trace barely moves — under a millivolt even at ten vesicles, because rest is already close to chloride's equilibrium. Inhibition mostly is not a push downward. It is a clamp: it holds the membrane where it is by opening a hole that resists being moved.

03 — blockers Cut the chain

Block the receptors and everything upstream still runs perfectly — calcium enters, vesicles fuse, transmitter fills the cleft — and the next neuron feels nothing. The signal is intact right up to the last step. That is where most synaptic drugs act.

04 — reuptake Stop the cleanup

Set calcium to 4, which is reliably subthreshold, then block reuptake and fire again. The same four vesicles now fire a spike. Nothing more was released — the transmitter simply stayed around, 33 ms instead of 7. This is roughly what an SSRI does, and why it works on release you already had.

A findingThe membrane is a coincidence detector

There is a result buried in this model that is worth stating plainly, because it contradicts the usual story. Textbooks describe postsynaptic potentials "summing until they reach threshold," which suggests a neuron patiently adding up inputs over time.

The Hodgkin–Huxley membrane does not do this. Fire the synapse repeatedly at 50 or 100 Hz and the cell never fires, no matter how many releases you stack up — each EPSP has decayed and the potassium conductance has pushed the membrane back before the next one lands. Only when releases arrive within about three milliseconds of each other do they add up enough to cross threshold.

This is a real property of the squid axon membrane, not an artefact: it accommodates, resisting slow depolarisation, and it fires on coincidence rather than on accumulation. Neurons that genuinely integrate over tens of milliseconds do it with different channels and a lot of dendrite — and the ones that need to detect simultaneity, like those computing sound direction from the two ears, look much more like this.

The parametersWhat the numbers are

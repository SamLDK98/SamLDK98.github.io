Field Theories

Overview

Field theories are mathematical frameworks where we imagine:

  • Every location in a space takes a value.
  • Objects in that space influence those values through one of their properties.

This framework is agnostic to specific systems of objects and environments.

Once we have a property that can be quantified and an environment that can interact with that property, we can describe it in the language of a field theory.

The clearest instantiation of this framework uses mass, because we can intuit the physical analogy. However, we will also think in terms of electrical charge to help intuit the framework abstractly.

Field Potential and Potential Energy

Imagine a 2D space. The yellow line represents the ground.

Potential, or Field Potential ($\Phi$), refers to the elevation of a particular point in this space relative to the ground (where $\Phi = 0$). It is a property of a location in an environment.

A coupling property is the property of an object that is sensitive to the field. For this gravitational analogy, that property is mass ($m$).

Potential energy ($U$), on the other hand, is a property of the interaction between the field potential and coupling property of the object.

You can think of the mass or field potential as scaling the other to give the potential energy.

$$\text{Potential Energy = Mass X Field Potential}$$ $$U = m\Phi$$

To move the ball up to a given height requires spending an amount of energy equal to the product of the ball's mass and the field potential of that elevation ($m\Phi$).

Once at that height, that exact amount of energy is stored as potential energy ($U$) by the ball-field system at that location.

Field Strength and Force

Let's imagine some 'land' in our space.

Imagine a slope running from the top left corner to the bottom right corner.

How do we describe the slope mathematically?

The $x$-axis denotes distance ($r$) while the $y$-axis denotes the field potential ($\Phi$).

The slope represents the change in field potential for a change in distance ($r$).

For a 1 unit change in distance, there is a -1 unit change in field potential.

$$\frac{-1}{1} = -1$$

The sign of the potential gradient tells us which way the potential increases; however, movement always occurs in the direction that potential decreases, so we must flip the sign.

$$-1 \rightarrow \ +1$$

The slope gradient with the sign flipped is called the Field Strength.

Field strength has no universal symbol, but in this scenario, field strength is gravity ($g$).

$$g=- \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta r}$$

Here, the slope is a straight line, so the field strength is the same at any point along the line; however, the slope will typically vary from point-to-point as the landscape becomes more complicated.

Field strength encodes two things: the direction and steepness of the slope at that particular point. It is therefore a vector rather than a scalar quantity.

In this 2D space, only one dimension is spatial ($r$), so the field can point in only one of two directions. Therefore, we can encode the direction in the sign of the field strength value. That is why we are not using vector arrow symbols.

  • $(+)$ means $\large \Phi$ is decreasing as we travel $\rightarrow$ along the $x$-axis.
  • $(-)$ means $\large \Phi$ is increasing as we travel $\rightarrow$ along the $x$-axis.

Remember that:

  • The field potential is a property of a location in the environment.
  • The coupling property of an object is sensitive to the properties of each location.

We have just established another location property: field strength.

Just as the coupling property scales the field potential to determine the value of a property of the object–field system (potential energy), it also scales the field strength to determine the value of another property of the object–field system: force.

$$F=mg$$

Now, let's introduce a blue ball to a location on the slope: $(1, 3)$.

The properties of the location are $\Phi = 3$ and $g = 1$.
The coupling property of the ball is $m = 2$.

The properties of the object-environment system are $U = 6$ and $F = 2$.

So, what does this force value mean for the object?

Force tells us the direction (via the sign) and strength of the push (number) on an object.

The object's mass is the resistance placed against the force. So depending on the mass, the acceleration of the object in response to the push varies.

$$a = \frac{F}{m} \qquad 1 = \frac{2}{2}$$

The key thing to realise:

The force that governs the object’s trajectory is exerted locally at each particular location. As the object moves, it encounters a continuous series of local gradients that influence its movement, rather than a force acting upon it from a distance.

Source Density

Let's start afresh with a new visualisation. I'm going to introduce some of the same concepts again.

Imagine a grid where every intersection has a value set to 0. We call this a field.

These values are sensitive to a particular property of objects: mass.

When an object is introduced, the values in the field at that location decrease in proportion to the mass of the object.

This effect propagates outward around the object, diminishing in strength the further it travels. The effect ultimately influences the entire field, never reaching exactly zero.

If we scattered many objects into the field, the values at the grid's intersections would naturally be affected by more than one mass.

At each intersection, all the contributions are summed to produce one net value.

These values at the intersections correspond to the elevation in our previous analogy. That is, they are field potentials.

We could think of high field potential values as being like the peaks of hills and the low field potential values being like the bottoms of valleys. In a gravitational field, positive masses create low regions called potential wells.

Naturally, slopes then exist—not in the physical sense, but in the mathematical sense—between the field potentials in our field.

The really interesting thing is that, although we might initially expect a random spread of numbers to appear in our field, resulting in jagged, zigzagging movement along the local gradients, we instead find much smoother, continuous changes in field potential across the landscape. It makes sense. A location $x$ beside a location $y$ is going to be affected by nearly all the exact same influences, except for a tiny difference.

This is an example of the classical superposition principle: in a linear system, the total effect at one specific location is the sum of all the individual contributions at that location.

Not to be confused with quantum superposition, where quantum states and their amplitudes are combined. Measurement probabilities are derived from those amplitudes; they are not simply summed like classical field contributions.

That means every point in our field also has a field strength, a single slope vector ($\vec{g}$) that indicates the direction and steepness of the slope it is a part of.

The other interesting thing to note here is that the environment itself (the scale of 'elevation' and the 'slopes' between peaks and troughs) is created by the objects. Well, not entirely. We should still think of the field as existing whether objects exist in it or not. The field describes the potential for interaction. But without objects it is entirely flat. Objects create the potential landscape, not the potential for a landscape!

A couple of refinements to our understanding...

I have been referring to 'objects' throughout this explanation. Yet, at the beginning I said only a particular property of an object was sensitive to the field. Thinking about objects feels more natural, but really it is exclusively the mass, charge, or whatever the coupling property may be that contributes to the field. Because it is these properties that create the potential landscape, we call them sources.

The object's mass (the source) is not concentrated in one point, but takes up a region of space. The same mass may take up a region of any size. The ratio of an object's mass to size (area or volume) is called source density ($\rho$).

The mass itself may be unevenly distributed throughout this region. So, to be a little more precise, we use pointwise source density to describe the mass per unit volume around each point. We can calculate the object’s total effect by adding, or integrating, the contributions from all these small regions.

$$\rho(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{dm}{dV}$$

The Solar System Collapse Thought Experiment

If you are pulling on a rope with someone and they let go, the end of the rope in your hand does not instantly go slack. The drop in tension actually travels down the rope until it reaches your end.

So, imagine the Sun suddenly vanishes. We would expect a delay before the tension of the 'pulling force' reaches the Earth.

However, Newton's inverse-square law for gravity had no variable for time. It predicted that the Earth would fly off instantaneously should the Sun vanish.

Time was considered absolute. "Now" happens at the same time at any location in the cosmos. Einstein's relativity was yet to come. So too was the understanding of light speed as the limiting speed for causal influence.

Still, Newton did not agree philosophically with the implication of his equation. He once wrote: "That one body may act upon another at a distance through a vacuum... is to me so great an absurdity that I believe no man who has in philosophical matters a competent faculty of thinking can ever fall into it."

Field theories were motivated in part to address this issue of 'action-at-a-distance'.

With a field, an object alters the space just next to it, which in turn alters the space next to it, and so forth. Action, then, only ever occurs locally, but propagates outwards to have effects at a distance.

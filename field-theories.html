Field Theories

Overview

Field theories are mathematical frameworks where we imagine:

  • Every location in a space takes a value.
  • Objects in that space influence those values through one of their properties.

This framework is agnostic to specific systems of objects and environments.

Once we have a property that can be quantified, and an environment that can interact with that property, we can describe it in the language of a field theory.

The clearest instantiation of this framework uses mass, because we can intuit the physical analogy. However, we will also think in terms of electrical charge to help intuit the framework abstractly.

Field Potential and Potential Energy

Imagine a 2D physical space. The yellow line represents the ground.

Potential, or Field Potential ($\Phi$), refers to the elevation of a particular point in this space relative to the ground (where $\Phi = 0$). It is a property of a location in an environment.

A coupling property is the property of an object that is sensitive to the field. For this gravitational analogy, that property is mass ($m$).

Potential energy ($U$), on the other hand, is a property of the interaction between both the field potential and coupling property of the object.

You can think of the mass or field potential as scaling the other to give the potential energy.

$$\text{Potential Energy = Mass X Field Potential}$$ $$U = m\Phi$$

To move the ball up to a given height requires spending an amount of energy equal to the ball's mass and the field potential of that elevation ($m\Phi$).

Once at that height, the ball holds that exact amount of energy as stored potential energy ($U$).

Field Strength and Force

Let's imagine some 'land' in our space.

Imagine a slope running from the top left corner to the bottom right corner.

How do we describe the slope mathematically?

The $x$-axis denotes distance ($r$) while the $y$-axis denotes the field potential ($\Phi$).

The slope represents the change in field potential for a change in distance ($r$).

For a 1 unit change in distance, there is a -1 unit change in field potential.

$$\frac{-1}{1} = -1$$

This ratio, at specific points in the space, is the local gradient of the field potential. Field Strength has the same steepness but points in the opposite direction, toward lower potential. In this example, the field strength is the same all along the slope because it is a straight line, but typically it will vary from point-to-point as the landscape becomes more complicated.

Field strength encodes two things: the direction and steepness of the slope at that particular point. It is therefore a vector rather than a scalar quantity. In this one-dimensional model of space, there are only two possible directions along $r$, so the sign of the field-strength value can encode the direction.

  • $(-)$ means the field strength points to the left $\leftarrow$
  • $(+)$ means the field strength points to the right $\rightarrow$

Field strength has no universal symbol, but in this scenario, field strength is gravity ($g$).

$$g=- \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta r}$$

Previously we said that field potential was a property of a location in the environment. The coupling property of an object was sensitive to the properties of each location.

This sensitivity meant the object's mass scaled the field potential to give potential energy.

We have now just established another property of each location: field strength. So, how does the coupling property of an object interact with this?

Likewise, the coupling property scales the field strength. This time, it determines the value of a new property of the interaction between the object and environment: force.

$$F=mg$$

Now, let's introduce a blue ball to a location on the slope: $(1, 3)$.

The properties of the location are: $\Phi = 3$ and $g = 1$.
The coupling property of the ball is: $m = 2$.

The properties of the object-environment system are: $U = 6$ and $F = 2$.

So, what does this force value mean for the object?

  • The force points in the positive $r$ direction and has magnitude $2$.
  • The ball accelerates in that direction at $a = F/m = 2/2 = 1$.

The key thing to realise:

What 'acts on' the ball to 'push' it in a specific direction and to accelerate it at a specific rate is right there at the specific location it exists at. In other words, there is not a force at the bottom of the slope pulling the ball down per se. Everything is encoded locally. The ball moves to the next point and that point acts on it according to its own properties. As such, the ball's movement is governed by a series of local gradients.

Source Density

Let's start afresh with a new visualisation. I'm going to introduce some of the same concepts again.

Imagine a grid where every intersection has a value set to 0. We call this a field.

These values are sensitive to a particular property of objects: mass.

When an object is introduced, the values in the field at that location decrease proportionally to the mass of the object.

This effect propagates outward around the object, diminishing in strength the further it travels.

If we scattered many objects into the field, the values at the grid's intersections would naturally be affected by more than one mass.

Each intersection sums all the effects on itself to produce a net value.

By the way, these values at the intersections are synonymous with the elevation in our previous analogy. That is, they are field potentials.

We could think of high field-potential values as being like the peaks of hills and low field-potential values as being like the bottoms of valleys.

Naturally, there then exist 'slopes'—not in the physical sense, but in the mathematical sense—between the field potentials in our field.

The really interesting thing is that, at first, we might expect a kind of random spread of numbers to appear in our field. In fact, because objects with larger masses make proportionally larger contributions to the field values, the combined numbers resolve into a coherent landscape in which slopes can be observed.

This is an instance of a fundamental law of physics known as the Principle of Classical Superposition: the total effect equals the sum of the individual parts. The term comes from the verb 'to superimpose'—to place one thing on top of another.

Not to be confused with quantum superposition, where quantum states and their amplitudes are combined. Measurement probabilities are derived from those amplitudes; they are not simply summed like classical field contributions.

That means every point in our field also has a field strength that indicates the direction and steepness of the slope it is a part of.

  • Globally: Across the entire field, the landscape is not a single uniform slope. It is a bumpy terrain filled with countless peaks, troughs, valleys, and saddle points.
  • Locally: At each specific point, the terrain at that exact coordinate resolves into a single slope vector ($\vec{g}$) with a direction and steepness.

The other interesting thing to note here is that the environment itself—the scale of 'elevation' and the 'slopes' between peaks and troughs—is created by the objects. Well, not entirely. We should still think of the field as existing whether objects exist in it or not. The field describes the potential for interaction. But without objects it is entirely flat. Objects create the landscape.

There are a couple of refinements to make to the understanding.

Objects here are referred to as sources.

A source does not only have mass, but size as well. It takes up some region of space. We could have a huge object with a mass of $x$ and a small object with a mass of $x$. The ratio of an object's mass to its volume is called the source density ($\rho$).

Because many objects are non-uniform in how their mass is distributed, the average density of the object overall may not reflect the source density at a specific point inside the object.

Instead, we use pointwise source density to measure the ratio of mass to volume in an infinitesimally small region:

$$\rho = \frac{dm}{dV}$$

The field starts as the primitive we assumed: every point reads exactly 0, and the cross-section beneath it is a flat line. Nothing is happening. Raising the mass places a source at the centre, and the landscape deforms around it.

  • Potential Φ is the landscape — the hills and valleys. A source digs a well, deepest at its centre and flattening back towards 0 as you move away.
  • Field strength |g| is the steepness of that landscape — the slope rather than the height.
  • Source density ρ is non-zero only where the object physically sits, and zero everywhere else. Switching to that view shows a flat disc and nothing beyond it.

That third view is the point your text is making: the density occupies the coordinates the object sits at, but those values are not the landscape. The landscape is what the density causes around it.

A consequenceWhy size matters, and where it doesn't

Because the source has real extent rather than being a point, two things follow that a zero-dimensional dot could never show you.

First, hold the mass at 100 and change only the size. The far field does not move at all — at a distance of 5 units the potential reads −20 and the strength reads 4 whether the source has a radius of 1, 2 or 3. Yet the density across those three cases differs by a factor of 27. From outside, you cannot tell how tightly the mass is packed. You can only see how much of it there is.

This is what "not directly observable" means

Source density leaves no fingerprint on the field outside the source. Two objects of the same mass and wildly different densities produce identical landscapes everywhere beyond their surfaces. The density only reveals itself inside — where the potential bottoms out, and how steeply the slope climbs to meet the surface.

Second, look at the field strength at the very centre of the source. It is zero. The slope of the landscape flattens out at the middle of the object, because the surrounding mass pulls equally in every direction and the pulls cancel. The strength climbs steadily from nothing at the centre to its maximum right at the surface, and only then begins to fall away. A point source has no inside, so it can never show you this.

Coordinates Away from the Green Line

In this graph, $r$ and $\Phi$ are not two independent spatial coordinates. The graph shows a function, $\Phi(r)$: each location $r$ has one field-potential value, represented by the green line.

On this line, $r = 3$ has $\Phi = 1$, while $r = 0$ has $\Phi = 4$. The points $(3,4)$ and $(0,2)$ do not lie on $\Phi(r)$, so they do not represent locations in this particular field plot. Field strength is therefore not defined at those off-line graph points.

In a genuinely two-dimensional physical space, potential would instead be a function $\Phi(x,y)$, and field strength would be defined at every spatial coordinate $(x,y)$ by the local gradient of that surface.

More Than One Spatial Direction

A true two-dimensional physical space already allows infinitely many directions, described using two independent components. In three-dimensional space, a field strength similarly has three independent components—one along each of the $x$, $y$, and $z$ axes.

We write this as $\mathbf{g} = -\nabla\Phi$. The three components combine to form one vector that can point in any spatial direction. A single plus or minus sign is no longer enough; the vector's components encode its full direction and magnitude.

Why the Minus Sign?

Gravity is not numerically negative in every coordinate system. The minus sign in $\mathbf{g} = -\nabla\Phi$ says that gravity points opposite to the direction in which potential increases—in other words, downhill toward lower potential.

Its numerical component may be positive or negative depending on which direction we chose as positive. In this diagram, $\Delta\Phi/\Delta r = -1$, so $g = -(-1) = +1$: gravity points toward increasing $r$, which is downhill on the plotted slope.

The Solar System Collapse Thought Experiment

If you are pulling on a rope with someone and they let go, the end of the rope in your hand does not instantly go slack. The drop in tension actually travels down the rope until it reaches your end.

So, imagine the Sun suddenly vanishes. We would expect a delay before the tension of the pulling force reaches the Earth.

However, Newton's equation for gravity had no variable for time. It therefore predicted that the Earth would fly off instantaneously should the Sun vanish.

Time was considered absolute. "Now" happens at the same time at any location in the cosmos. Einstein's relativity was yet to come. So too was the understanding of light speed as the fastest anything could travel.

Still, Newton did not agree philosophically with the implication of his equation. He once wrote: "That one body may act upon another at a distance through a vacuum... is to me so great an absurdity that I believe no man who has in philosophical matters a competent faculty of thinking can ever fall into it."

Field theories were motivated in part to address this issue of 'action-at-a-distance'.

With a field, an object alters the space just next to it, which in turn alters the space next to it, and so forth. Action, then, only ever occurs locally, but propagates outwards to have effects at a distance.

Complex Numbers · Chapter 4

Polar Form

Two Ways to Describe Points in a Plane

Cartesian Coordinate System

As you already know, we can describe points in terms of how far we need to move along the $x$-axis and how far along the $y$-axis.

Polar Form

However, there is an alternative.

We could describe any point in a plane in terms of the angle we need to take and the distance we then need to extend out.

Polar Form

Multiplication on the real number line affects a number's distance from the origin (magnitude).

For example, multiplying by $3$ triples the distance from the origin:

$$2 \rightarrow 6$$

Complex multiplication affects both distance and direction from the origin.

The distance of a complex number from the origin is called its modulus, written as:

$$|z|$$

When describing complex numbers geometrically, we often call this distance $r$.

The direction is captured by the angle between the positive real axis and the line from the origin to the point. This angle is often called the argument of the complex number.

So instead of describing a complex number by its real and imaginary components, we can describe it by its distance and direction:

$$a + bi=(r,\theta)$$

These are called polar coordinates.

Converting from complex to polar form is straightforward; however, converting from polar to complex form can be more challenging.

From $a + bi$ to $(r, \theta)$

Suppose we begin with: $3+4i$.

The real and imaginary parts tell us that the point lies at: $(3,4)$.

The distance from the origin is: $r=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5$.

The angle can be found from the right triangle:

$$\tan\theta=\frac{4}{3}$$

So:

$$\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)$$

Therefore:

$$\theta \approx 53.1^\circ$$

So the complex number:

$$3+4i$$

can also be described in polar form as:

$$(5,53.1^\circ)$$

From $(r, \theta)$ to $a + bi$

Now suppose we know $(r, \theta)$ is $(5, 53.1^\circ)$.

How do we convert from $(r, \theta)$ to $a + bi$?

This is exactly the problem solved earlier by trigonometry.

Remember that any position on the unit circle can also be represented by an angle $\theta$, because the functions sine and cosine map that angle to its corresponding $x$- and $y$-coordinates: $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$.

The unit circle has a radius of 1. To reach points elsewhere in the plane, we keep the same angle but extend or shrink the radius.

This can be thought of as first moving around the unit circle to choose a direction, and then moving further away from or closer to the origin along that direction.

If the radius is $r$, then both coordinates scale proportionally:

$$x = r \cos \theta \quad y = r \sin \theta$$

So any point can be represented as:

$$(r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta)$$

The point, the real axis, and the radius form a right triangle.

From the unit circle we know:

  • $x=r\cos\theta$
  • $y=r\sin\theta$

Therefore:

  • $a=r\cos\theta$
  • $b=r\sin\theta$.

Substituting these into $a+bi$ gives: $z = r\cos\theta + ri\sin\theta$

Factoring out $r$: $z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$

Multiplying Complex Numbers in Polar Form

$$z_1 = r_1(\cos\theta_1 + i\sin\theta_1)$$

$$z_2 = r_2(\cos\theta_2 + i\sin\theta_2)$$

When multiplied together:

  • The distances from the origin (magnitudes) multiply: $r_1r_2$.
  • The angles add: $\theta_1 + \theta_2$.

Multiplying by a complex number stretches by a certain amount and rotates by a certain angle.

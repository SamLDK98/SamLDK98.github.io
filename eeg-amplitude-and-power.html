Amplitude and Power

What Is Amplitude?

An EEG electrode measures the voltage at a point on the scalp relative to a reference. That reading changes from moment to moment. Imagine watching it rise from 0 to +5 µV, fall through 0 to −5 µV, and return to 0. If that pattern repeats, we see the same sequence again and again.

\[0 \;\to\; \class{mjx-amp}{+5} \;\to\; 0 \;\to\; \class{mjx-amp}{-5} \;\to\; 0 \;\to\; \cdots\]

The highest and lowest readings are both 5 µV away from the centre line. That distance is the amplitude.

More generally, amplitude describes the maximum displacement of an oscillation from its equilibrium. In the reading above, the equilibrium is the centre line at 0 µV.

Sinusoidal waves consist of the same oscillation repeating, so the amplitude of the wave is also the amplitude of each oscillation.

If the amplitude of the wave is 5 µV and the equilibrium is at 0 µV, every peak reaches +5 µV and every trough reaches −5 µV. The distance from peak to trough is 10 µV.

Now show the record. The graph is the same number written down over time: each point is one reading, placed at the moment it was taken. The wave you see in an EEG trace is this record.

Every cycle of a pure sinusoid has the same amplitude. A raw EEG trace can contain several rhythms, and its peaks and troughs can vary from cycle to cycle. When we talk about the amplitude of a rhythm in that trace, we can estimate it for a particular stretch of the recording and see how it changes over time.

What Does the Wave Describe in EEG?

Scalp EEG measures the voltage difference between an electrode and a reference. Much of the signal comes from the combined electrical effects of postsynaptic currents, which flow when neurons receive input.

  • Frequency counts how often the voltage pattern repeats. A 10 Hz rhythm completes ten cycles per second. Individual neurons can fire at different rates and may fire during only some cycles of that rhythm.
  • Phase gives the position within a cycle. Two 10 Hz rhythms can have the same frequency and reach their peaks at different times. If the peak of one lines up with the trough of the other, they are half a cycle apart.
  • Amplitude gives the size of the recorded voltage swings. It depends on the strength of the contributing sources, their distance and orientation relative to the electrodes, and how closely their cycles line up.

How Synchronisation Changes Amplitude

Move the slider to change how closely the eight source waves line up. Each source represents the voltage that a similarly oriented group of neurons would contribute. They all have the same frequency and amplitude throughout.

At one end, peaks and troughs from different sources overlap and cancel. At the other, the peaks arrive together and so do the troughs. The combined amplitude grows even though the number and strength of the sources stay the same.

From Amplitude to Power

Over a full cycle, a sine wave centred on zero has an average voltage of zero. Its positive and negative voltages cancel in the average, however large the swings are.

To calculate the signal’s power over a stretch of EEG, square each voltage sample and then average those squares. Squaring makes a sample of −5 µV contribute the same amount as a sample of +5 µV. If the voltages are measured in microvolts, power is measured in square microvolts (µV²).

For a pure sinusoid centred on zero, the power over a whole number of cycles is half the squared amplitude, A² / 2. So a 5 µV sinusoid has a power of 12.5 µV², and a 10 µV sinusoid has a power of 50 µV². Doubling the amplitude quadruples the power.

Power Across Frequencies

Power gives us one number for a stretch of EEG. What if we want to know how much of that power comes from activity around 10 Hz? The power spectral density shows how power is distributed across frequencies, in µV² per Hz. The area under that curve across a range of frequencies gives the power in that band.

Polynomials

Constructing Classes of Equations

The General Forms of Equations

We want to classify equations according to shared structures.

For each class we will:

  • Identify the general structure that every equation in the class can be simplified to.
  • Rearrange that structure algebraically to create a general solution formula.

We'll build increasingly complicated classes of equations, adding one new ingredient at a time.

An Unknown = A Known

What is the simplest equation?

\[x=7\]

By definition it is already solved.

But for fun, let's identify the structure. If \(x\) is the unknown and \(c\) is a known constant:

\[x=c\]

The general structure and the formula are the same thing.

Okay, that is a bit silly. Let's introduce something new.

An Unknown, Constants, and Addition

Addition of constants permitted

Let's now allow:

  • One unknown.
  • Any number of constants.
  • Addition.

(Subtraction is included in addition as it involves the addition of a negative number.)

\[\begin{aligned} x+2&=7\\ x+2+3&=7+10\\ x+2+3+4+2&=3+7+10+6 \end{aligned}\]

To find the structure these share, we need one idea:

Any number of like terms can be combined into a single term.

Constants are like terms: the sum of any set of constants is always another constant.

\[\begin{array}{r} 2 + 3 = 5 \\ 2 + 3 + 4 = 9 \\ 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 = 20 \end{array}\]

So however many constants sit on one side of an equation, we can always replace them with a single symbol.

Call the sum of all constants on the left hand side \(b\), and the sum of all constants on the right hand side \(c\).

\(x\) is not a like term, so it can't be merged into \(b\).

We could represent all of these equations algebraically as:

\[x+b=c\]

Rearranging to express \(x\) in terms of the known quantities gives the general solution formula:

\[x=c-b\]

Adding copies of the unknown

Let's expand the inclusion criteria:

  • Any number of copies of the unknown.
  • Any number of constants.
  • Addition.
\[x+x+3=7\]
\[x+x+x+x+3+4=7+13\]
\[x+x+x+x+x+x+x+10=150\]

Copies of \(x\) are like terms too, so they can be combined into a single term: \(2x\), \(4x\), \(7x\).

The general structure under this expanded criteria is therefore:

\[ax+b=c\]

The unknown now has a coefficient, \(a\): for now, the number of copies of \(x\). (Later a coefficient can be any known number, such as \(-3\) or \(\frac{1}{2}\).)

The previous structure, \(x+b=c\), is just the special case \(a=1\).

Each new structure can capture every expression the previous ones did.

The unknown can appear on both sides, too:

\[3x+2=x+10\]

Subtracting \(x\) from both sides reveals our structure: \(2x+2=10\).

This is our first real class of equations: linear equations.

The formula for linear equations is the one we've already met:

\[x=\frac{c-b}{a} \qquad (a\neq 0)\]

Linear expressions

Pause for a moment to make sure you really appreciate this:

  • We could take any number of constants of any values (provided they are real numbers).
  • And any number of copies of the unknown.
  • Allow the operation of addition.
  • And we can always simplify to the structure \(ax+b\) (and this is enabled because we can combine like terms.)

Adding two linear expressions just combines the like terms again.

In general:

\[(a_1x+b_1)+(a_2x+b_2)=(a_1+a_2)x+(b_1+b_2)\]

The result is another linear expression, or something even simpler: if the \(x\) terms cancel, only a constant is left.

\[(2x+1)+(-2x+3)=4\]

So addition never creates a new kind of term. It can only keep the kinds we already have, or lose one.

Linear expressions will matter in a moment, when we start multiplying.

An Unknown, Constants, Addition, and Multiplication

Can we also allow multiplication and keep the same general structure?

\[ax+b=c\]

A product fits this structure only if it gives terms that are like the ones already in it: a constant, or a number of copies of \(x\).

Here are the products we could form from the things we have so far:

ProductExampleFits \(ax+b=c\)?
1Constant × constant\(3\cdot2=6\)✓
2Constant × the unknown\(5\cdot x=5x\)✓
3Constant × linear expression\(4(2x+5)=8x+20\)✓
4The unknown × the unknown\(x\cdot x=x^2\)✗
5The unknown × linear expression\(x(3x+8)=3x^2+8x\)✗
6Linear expression × linear expression\((2x+1)(x+4)=2x^2+9x+4\)✗

So what's going on here?

The distributive law

Cases 3, 5 and 6 multiply sums. The distributive law says that multiplying a sum means multiplying each of its terms:

\[p(q+r)=pq+pr\]

Applying it breaks every product of sums into a sum of products of single terms, and each of those is case 1, 2 or 4:

\[\begin{aligned} 4(2x+5)&=\underbrace{4\cdot2x}_{\text{case 2}}+\underbrace{4\cdot5}_{\text{case 1}}=8x+20\\[4pt] x(3x+8)&=\underbrace{x\cdot3x}_{\text{case 4}}+\underbrace{x\cdot8}_{\text{case 2}}=3x^2+8x\\[4pt] (2x+1)(x+4)&=\underbrace{2x\cdot x}_{\text{case 4}}+\underbrace{2x\cdot4}_{\text{case 2}}+\underbrace{1\cdot x}_{\text{case 2}}+\underbrace{1\cdot4}_{\text{case 1}}\\[4pt] &=2x^2+9x+4 \end{aligned}\]

This is why distribution matters to our classification. Without it, every new way of multiplying brackets would be a new case to think about. With it, we only ever need to know how to multiply single terms.

It also explains the pattern of ticks and crosses. Cases 3, 5 and 6 inherit their answer from the single products inside them: case 3 contains only cases 1 and 2, while cases 5 and 6 each contain an \(x\) times an \(x\), which is case 4.

Multiplying single terms

So everything comes down to one question: what happens when single terms are multiplied?

Adding copies of \(x\) changes the coefficient:

\[x+x=2x\qquad x+x+x=3x\qquad x+x+x+x=4x\]

Multiplying copies of \(x\) changes the exponent:

\[x\cdot x=x^2\qquad x\cdot x\cdot x=x^3\qquad x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x=x^4\]

When two single terms are multiplied, the numbers multiply and the copies of \(x\) pile up, so the exponents add:

\[(3x^2)(5x)=3\cdot5\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x=15x^3\]

A product that comes out as a constant or as copies of \(x\) can be absorbed into a term we already have. But \(x\cdot x\) can't: \(x^2\) is not a constant and not a number of copies of \(x\).

And terms with different exponents are unlike terms, so they can never be combined:

\[3x+2x=5x\qquad\text{but}\qquad x^3+x^2\ \text{can't be simplified}\]

Quadratic equations

Once multiplication is allowed, an equation can contain three kinds of term: \(x^2\) terms, \(x\) terms and constants.

Distribute any brackets, combine like terms of each kind, and every such equation has at most one term of each kind on each side.

From here on it's simpler to move everything to one side, leaving \(0\) on the other. The general structure becomes:

\[ax^2+bx+c=0\qquad(a\neq0)\]

A note on letters: in this form \(c\) is the constant term on the left, not the right-hand side as before. This is the conventional way to write it. The linear structure can be moved over in the same way: \(ax+b=c\) becomes \(ax+(b-c)=0\), and since \(b-c\) is just another constant, we can write it as \(ax+b=0\).

We call this class quadratic equations. (The condition \(a\neq0\) just says the \(x^2\) term is really there. If it isn't, the equation is linear.)

Creating the formula for quadratics is not as straightforward as it was for linear equations. The last part of this section explains why, and the general formula gets a section of its own.

And nothing stops us multiplying more copies of \(x\): \(x^3\), \(x^4\), and so on. Each new exponent is a new kind of term, and a new class of equation.

Polynomial Equations

Let's take stock. We allow one unknown, any constants, and addition, subtraction and multiplication, as often as we like, on either side.

Two moves will always simplify such an equation:

  1. Distribute, until no products of sums are left. Every term is now a single number times some number of copies of \(x\).
  2. Combine like terms, until there is at most one term for each exponent.

Moving everything to one side then gives the general structure of every equation we can build:

\[a_nx^n+\dots+a_2x^2+a_1x+a_0=0\]

The left-hand side is a polynomial: a sum of terms, each a constant times a whole-number power of \(x\).

  • Each \(a_kx^k\) is a term, and \(a_k\) is its coefficient.
  • \(a_0\), the term with no \(x\), is the constant term.
  • The highest exponent with a non-zero coefficient is the degree.

Every class we built is the family of polynomial equations of one degree:

DegreeNameGeneral structure
1Linear\(ax+b=0\)
2Quadratic\(ax^2+bx+c=0\)
3Cubic\(ax^3+bx^2+cx+d=0\)
4Quartic\(ax^4+bx^3+cx^2+dx+e=0\)

Simplify first, then classify

We can only read off the class of an equation after distributing and combining like terms. Before that, it can look like something it isn't:

\[x(x+3)=x^2+5\]

This looks quadratic. Distribute:

\[x^2+3x=x^2+5\]

Now take \(x^2\) from both sides, and the \(x^2\) terms cancel:

\[3x=5\]

It was a linear equation all along.

This shows what each move does to the degree:

  • Distributing never changes the degree. It only changes how the expression is written. When single terms multiply, their exponents add, so a product's degree is the sum of its factors' degrees: linear × linear is quadratic.
  • Combining like terms can lower the degree, but only when the highest terms cancel, as \(x^2\) did here. It can never raise it.

Sums and Products: Two Ways to Write the Same Thing

Distributing turns a product into a sum of powers:

\[(x-1)(x+7)=x^2+7x-x-7=x^2+6x-7\]

Both sides are the same polynomial, written two ways. Each way is good for something different.

The sum of powers is the form for classifying. The degree and the coefficients can be read straight off it, so it tells us which class an equation is in, and its coefficients are exactly what a general solution formula takes as inputs.

The product is the form for solving. If a product is zero, one of its factors must be zero:

\[(x-1)(x+7)=0\quad\longrightarrow\quad x-1=0\ \text{ or }\ x+7=0\quad\longrightarrow\quad x=1\ \text{ or }\ x=-7\]

In the sum form, the same equation is \(x^2+6x-7=0\), and it is much less obvious what \(x\) is.

The reason connects back to the previous page: solving means isolating the unknown.

In a linear equation, combining like terms gathers every copy of \(x\) into a single term, so there is one place to isolate it from.

In \(x^2+6x-7=0\), the unknown is spread across two unlike terms, \(x^2\) and \(6x\), that can never be combined. There is no single place to isolate it from.

The product form gathers the unknown back up. In \((x-1)(x+7)=0\), each factor contains \(x\) just once, and each can be solved on its own.

So distributing and combining like terms take us towards the general structure, which is what we need to classify. But solving asks us to go the other way: back from the sum to a form where \(x\) appears once.

Distributing is always mechanical. Going back is not, and that is why quadratics need more than rearranging. One way back, which works for every quadratic, is Completing the Square: it rewrites \(x^2+6x-7=0\) as \((x+3)^2=16\), where \(x\) appears once.

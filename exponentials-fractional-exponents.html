Exponentials

Fractional Exponents

Understanding the rational exponent rule is essential for understanding exponents.

\[\boxed{a^{\class{mjx-exp}{\frac{m}{n}}}=(\sqrt[n]{a})^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}}}\]

Let's first understand why:

\[a^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{a}\]

The power of a power rule reveals why.

\[(a^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}})^{\class{mjx-exp}{n}}=a^{\class{mjx-exp}{mn}}\]

Imagine:

\[16^{\frac{1}{2}}\]

If we raise this to the power of two:

\[(16^{\frac{1}{2}})^2\]

The exponents multiply:

\[\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 = 1\]

So the term equals \(16\):

\[(16^{\frac{1}{2}})^2 = 16\]

That means that whatever the value of \(16^{\frac{1}{2}}\) is, when it is squared, it must equal \(16\).

It therefore must be the square root of \(16\), i.e. \(4\).

Generally, raising a number to the power of \(\frac{1}{2}\) is equivalent to taking its principal square root.

\[\boxed{a^{\frac{1}{2}} = \sqrt{a}}\]

The deeper thing to realise is what it means to say that exponentiation is multiplicative.

Think about the distance between \(1\) and \(100\).

We start at \(1\) rather than \(0\): multiplying \(0\) by anything leaves \(0\), so we could never move away from it by multiplying.

If we were to move additively across the number line from left to right, each step would cover the same distance. We travel \(1\) unit at a time.

If we were to move multiplicatively, the steps are not spaced evenly.

Each step multiplies by the same factor, so each one covers more distance than the step before.

Take two equal steps from \(1\) to \(100\):

\[1 \overset{+49.5}{\longrightarrow} 50.5 \overset{+49.5}{\longrightarrow} 100\]
\[1 \overset{\times 10}{\longrightarrow} 10 \overset{\times 10}{\longrightarrow} 100\]

Adding, the halfway point is \(50.5\). Multiplying, it is \(10\): the number that gives \(100\) when multiplied by itself.

\[100^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{100}=10\]

An exponent of \(\frac{1}{2}\) means halfway in steps, not halfway in distance.

If \(x=8\):

When we are moving from \(x \to 2x\), we are moving from \(8 \to 16\).

But how does this change occur along the journey from \(8 \to 16\)?

It occurs at a constant rate.

It changes additively.

The difference in value between moving from \(1.2x \to 1.4x\) is the same as moving from \(1.7x \to 1.9x\).

It's \(+1.6\) in both cases.

The change occurs at a constant ratio.

It changes multiplicatively.

The difference between \(x^{\class{mjx-exp}{1.2}} \to x^{\class{mjx-exp}{1.4}}\):

\[51.984 - 34.297 = \boxed{17.687}\]

Although the exponent increases by exactly \(0.2\) in both cases, the absolute increase in the output is much larger in the second case.

However, the ratio between the two is identical:

\[\frac{18.379}{12.126} \approx 1.5157\]
\[\frac{51.984}{34.297} \approx 1.5157\]

The Halfway Point of an Additive Versus a Multiplicative Journey

Looking at the value of the halfway point (in terms of steps) highlights the difference in how these processes grow.

Additive growth: \(8 \to 16\).

The halfway point was \(1.5x\) with a value of \(12\).

\(12\) is halfway between \(8\) and \(16\).

Multiplicative growth: \(8 \to 64\).

The halfway point was \(x^{\class{mjx-exp}{1.5}}\) with a value of approximately \(22.627\).

So when we are halfway in terms of multiplicative steps, we are not halfway in terms of value.

However, look at how much we have multiplied \(8\) by to reach \(22.627\):

\[\frac{22.627}{8} \approx \boxed{2.828}\]

What happens if we multiply \(22.627\) by the same thing we multiplied \(8\) by?

\[22.627 \times 2.828 \approx 63.99\]

(If our factors were more precisely approximated this would be \(64\).)

So in terms of multiplicative steps, we are in fact halfway to \(64\) when we are at \(22.627\)!

The arithmetic midpoint divides the difference into two equal parts.

The geometric midpoint divides the ratio into two equal factors.

Divided into Two Equal Factors? That Sounds Like a Square Root…

Taking roots finds the value that, multiplied by itself a given number of times, produces the target number.

The second root of \(9\) is the number that multiplied by itself twice gives \(9\).

\(3\) is the geometric midpoint between \(1\) and \(9\).

Raising \(9\) to the power \(0.5\) (or as we would typically write it, \(9^{\class{mjx-exp}{\frac{1}{2}}}\)) therefore gives the square root of the number.

A fractional exponent with \(1\) in the numerator is asking for the number that, when multiplied by itself the number of times specified in the denominator, gives the base.

We are dividing the multiplicative change into smaller, equal multiplicative steps.

  • \(x^{\class{mjx-exp}{\frac{1}{2}}}\) finds the square root.
  • \(x^{\class{mjx-exp}{\frac{1}{3}}}\) finds the cube root.
  • \(x^{\class{mjx-exp}{\frac{1}{8}}}\) finds the value that, when multiplied by itself \(8\) times, gives \(x\).

This All Follows from the Power of a Power Rule

The power of a power rule can further help illustrate why fractional exponents work the way they do.

Remember:

\[(a^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}})^{\class{mjx-exp}{n}}=a^{\class{mjx-exp}{mn}}\]

Imagine:

\[16^{\frac{1}{2}}\]

If we raise this to the power of two:

\[(16^{\frac{1}{2}})^2\]

The exponents multiply:

\[\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 = 1\]

So the term equals \(16\):

\[(16^{\frac{1}{2}})^2 = 16\]

That means that whatever the value of \(16^{\frac{1}{2}}\) is, when it is squared, it must equal \(16\). So it must be \(\sqrt{16}=4\).

The Unit Fraction Exponent

Let's now formalise what we have conceptually covered.

When we raise a number to a unit fraction, we are looking for the number that, when multiplied by itself the number of times denoted by the denominator, gives the base.

This is exactly the same as taking the root of the number, with the denominator as the index in radical notation.

\[\boxed{a^{\class{mjx-exp}{1/n}} = \sqrt[n]{a}}\]

But what about when we have a numerator that is not \(1\)?

The Rational Exponent Rule

\[\boxed{a^{\class{mjx-exp}{\frac{m}{n}}}=(\sqrt[n]{a})^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}}}\]

Consider:

\[16^{\class{mjx-exp}{\frac{3}{4}}}\]

The denominator tells us how many multiplicative steps to divide the journey from \(1\) to \(16\) into.

In this case:

  • The journey is from \(1 \to 16\).
  • There are \(4\) multiplicative steps to be taken: \(x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x = 16\).

If the numerator was \(1\), then we would just be looking for the fourth root of \(16\):

\[x^4 = 16\]
\[x = \sqrt[4]{16}\]
\[x = 2\]

The resulting value is the ratio by which we must multiply to reach \(16\) in four steps from \(1\).

The numerator indicates how many of these steps to take.

If the exponent was \(\frac{4}{4}\), then we would be taking four of these multiplicative steps and would reach \(16\).

This makes sense, as \(\frac{4}{4} = 1\):

\[16^{\class{mjx-exp}{1}}=16\]

When the numerator is \(1\), we are taking only one multiplicative step.

The first step multiplies \(1\) by the common ratio, so it will always just equal the common ratio.

\[16^{\class{mjx-exp}{\frac{1}{4}}} = 2\]

When the numerator is \(3\), it means we must take three multiplicative steps.

That's \(1 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8\).

We can express this by finding the common ratio first, and then raising it to the exponent that counts how many times we multiply by it.

So, to find the common ratio:

\[\sqrt[4]{16}=2\]

To multiply by the common ratio three times:

\[(\sqrt[4]{16})^{\class{mjx-exp}{3}}\]

This gives:

\[2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8\]

The denominator determines the common ratio of the steps.

The numerator determines how many of those steps we take.

\[\boxed{a^{\class{mjx-exp}{\frac{m}{n}}}=(\sqrt[n]{a})^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}}}\]

What if the numerator is greater than the denominator?

Consider:

\[16^{\class{mjx-exp}{\frac{5}{4}}}\]

We still divide the multiplicative journey into fourth-root steps, each with a ratio of \(2\).

But now we take five steps rather than four:

\[1 \to 2 \to 4 \to 8 \to 16 \to 32\]

Therefore:

\[16^{\class{mjx-exp}{\frac{5}{4}}} = 32\]

This demonstrates something important: fractional exponents do not necessarily produce smaller values. They specify a number of multiplicative steps, which may be fewer or greater than the number needed to reach the base.

Exponentials

Fractional Exponents

What happens to the value of a term as we move continuously between two coefficients?

Increasing a coefficient by a whole number adds that many copies of \(x\) to the sum.

\[3x = x + x + x\]
\[(\class{mjx-value}{1}+3)x = x + x + x + \class{mjx-value}{x}\]

Increasing a coefficient by a fraction adds that fraction of \(x\) to the sum.

\[(\class{mjx-value}{0.25}+3)x = x + x + x + \class{mjx-value}{\frac{x}{4}}\]

Notice that multiplication by a fraction corresponds to division.

\[\begin{aligned} 0.10 \times 50 &= 5 & \qquad \qquad \frac{50}{10} &= 5 \\[0.6em] 0.5 \times 14 &= 7 & \qquad \qquad \frac{14}{2} &= 7 \\[0.6em] 0.125 \times 24 &= 3 & \qquad \qquad \frac{24}{8} &= 3 \end{aligned}\]

So a fractional coefficient essentially performs division on the variable and then adds the quotient to the sum.

To find the value of \(6.43x\) when \(x=2\):

\[6 \cdot 2 = 12\]
\[\frac{2}{100} \cdot 43 = 0.86\]
\[12 + 0.86 = 12.86\]

What happens to the value of a power as we move continuously between two exponents?

Increasing an exponent by a whole number adds another factor of \(x\) to the product.

\[x^3 = x \cdot x \cdot x\]
\[x^{3+\class{mjx-value}{1}} = x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot \class{mjx-value}{x}\]

We might then think that adding a fraction to the exponent would result in including that fraction of \(x\) into the product.

But that would be treating our powers like additive growth.

Imagine if increasing an exponent by a fraction meant we included that fraction of \(x\).

\[x^{(3\,+\,0.5)} = x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot \class{mjx-wrong}{\frac{x}{2}}\]

Say \(x=10\):

\[10^{(3\,+\,0.5)} = 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot \class{mjx-wrong}{5}\]

Now imagine we added another \(\frac{1}{2}\) to the exponent.

\[10^{(3\,+\,0.5\,+\,0.5)} = 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot \class{mjx-wrong}{5} \cdot \class{mjx-wrong}{5}\]

Yikes! Now we have ended up multiplying by \(25\) while only increasing the exponent by \(1\):

\[10^{(3\,+\,1)} = 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot \class{mjx-wrong}{25}\]

We forgot to appreciate that our exponents correspond to multiplicative growth.

But one exponent contributes \(\times 8\) not \(+8\) to the output.

A fractional increase refers then to multiplicative growth.

For example, \(x^{\frac{1}{2}}\) asks for the factor that when multiplied by itself twice gives \(x\).

\[a \cdot a = x\]
\[a = \sqrt{x}\]

That factor is then included in the product.

\[5^{4.5}\]
\[5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 \cdot \sqrt{5}\]

In general, raising a base to \(\frac{1}{n}\) gives the factor that when multiplied by itself \(n\) times gives the base.

\[a^{\frac{1}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{a}\]

Notice that exponentiation by a fraction corresponds to radication (taking roots).

Both seek the factor when we must reach a target by multiplication of an identical factor given a specific number of steps.

\(x^{\frac{1}{2}} \rightarrow\) finds the square root \(\rightarrow \sqrt[2]{x}\).

\(x^{\frac{1}{3}} \rightarrow\) finds the cube root \(\rightarrow \sqrt[3]{x}\).

\(x^{\frac{1}{8}} \rightarrow\) finds the value that when multiplied by itself \(8\) times gives \(x \rightarrow \sqrt[8]{x}\).

But what about when we have a numerator that is not \(1\)?

With \(1\) in the numerator:

\[8^{\frac{1}{10}}\]

This is the value that, when multiplied by itself \(10\) times, gives \(8\).

With a number greater than \(1\) in the numerator:

\[8^{\frac{3}{10}}\]

This is the value that, when multiplied by itself \(10\) times, gives \(8\), then multiplied by itself \(3\) times.

The value that, when multiplied by itself \(10\) times, gives \(8\):

\[8^{\frac{1}{10}}\]

Then multiplied by itself \(3\) times:

\[8^{\frac{1}{10}} \times 8^{\frac{1}{10}} \times 8^{\frac{1}{10}}\]

Just as in division we first find the value of one step and then scale it by multiplication, here we find the value of one step by radication and then scale it via exponentiation.

To find the value of \(2^{6.43}\):

\[2^6 = 64\]
\[2^{0.43}\]

We want to break the journey up into \(100\) steps of equal ratios:

\[\sqrt[100]{2}\]

And then take \(43\) steps to find our combined factor. This is done by multiplying this factor by itself \(43\) times.

\[\left(\sqrt[100]{2}\right)^{43} \approx 1.347\]

So we have a product that looks like:

\[2^{6.43} = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1.347\]

Or in other words:

\[64 \cdot 1.347 \approx 86.22\]

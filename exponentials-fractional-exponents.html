Exponentials

Fractional Exponents

Understanding the rational exponent rule is essential for understanding exponents.

\[\boxed{a^{\class{mjx-exp}{\frac{m}{n}}}=(\sqrt[n]{a})^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}}}\]

Let's first understand why:

\[a^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{a}\]

The power of a power rule reveals why.

\[(a^{\class{mjx-exp}{m}})^{\class{mjx-exp}{n}}=a^{\class{mjx-exp}{mn}}\]

Imagine:

\[16^{\frac{1}{2}}\]

If we raise this to the power of two:

\[(16^{\frac{1}{2}})^2\]

The exponents multiply:

\[\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 = 1\]

So the term equals \(16\):

\[(16^{\frac{1}{2}})^2 = 16\]

That means that whatever the value of \(16^{\frac{1}{2}}\) is, when it is squared, it must equal \(16\).

It therefore must be the square root of \(16\), i.e. \(4\).

Generally, raising a number to the power of \(\frac{1}{2}\) is equivalent to taking its principal square root.

\[\boxed{a^{\frac{1}{2}} = \sqrt{a}}\]

The deeper thing to realise is what it means to say that exponentiation is multiplicative.

Think about getting from \(1\) to \(100\) in equal steps. (We start at \(1\) rather than \(0\): multiplying \(0\) by anything leaves \(0\).)

If the steps are additive, each step adds the same amount. Four steps of \(+24.75\) land on \(25.75\), \(50.5\), \(75.25\) and \(100\). They are evenly spaced.

If the steps are multiplicative, each step multiplies by the same factor. Four steps of \(\times\sqrt{10}\) (about \(3.16\)) land on about \(3.16\), \(10\), \(31.6\) and \(100\). The steps are equal, but each one covers more distance than the step before.

Halfway through the steps, adding has reached \(50.5\). Multiplying has reached only \(10\).

An exponent counts multiplicative steps.

\(100^0=1\) is the start, with no steps taken. \(100^1=100\) is one whole step: \(\times100\).

So \(100^{\frac{1}{2}}\) is half of that step: a factor that, taken twice, makes \(\times100\). By the product rule:

\[100^{\frac{1}{2}}\times100^{\frac{1}{2}}=100^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}=100^1=100\]

That factor is \(10\), the square root of \(100\).

As the exponent moves evenly from \(0\) to \(1\), the value does not. It creeps up to \(10\) by the halfway point, then covers the remaining \(90\) in the second half.

An exponent of \(\frac{1}{2}\) means halfway in steps, not halfway in distance.

Polynomials

Addition and Multiplication of Unknowns

Is Multiplication Just Repeated Addition?

Addition combines quantities into one:

\[2+3=5\]

Multiplication is usually introduced as repeated addition:

\[5\cdot3=5+5+5=3+3+3+3+3=15\]

So it can feel like multiplication is addition in disguise, and should behave the same way.

When we multiply by a positive whole number, it is. But once negative numbers are allowed, multiplication does something addition never does. That difference is where quadratics get their two solutions.

Sliding, Stretching and Flipping

Picture every number on the number line, and apply one operation to all of them at once.

Adding \(3\) slides every number \(3\) places to the right. Every number moves the same distance, in the same direction, wherever it starts.

Multiplying by \(3\) works differently. It moves each number to \(3\) times its distance from \(0\), on the same side: \(2\) goes to \(6\), and \(-4\) goes to \(-12\). Numbers right of \(0\) move right, numbers left of \(0\) move left, and \(0\) stays put.

Multiplying by \(-3\) stretches in the same way, but also flips every number to the other side of \(0\): \(2\) goes to \(-6\), and \(-4\) goes to \(12\).

Repeated addition explains the stretch, but not the flip: there is no way to add something "negative three times". The flip is the new ingredient. It is also why a negative times a negative is positive: \((-3)(-4)\) flips \(-4\), which is on the left, over to the right, and stretches it to \(12\).

So addition slides. Multiplication stretches from \(0\), and flips across \(0\) when the multiplier is negative. Multiplication is anchored at \(0\) in a way addition isn't.

Why Linear Equations Have One Solution

Watch the order of the numbers. After adding \(3\), they are in the same order as before. After multiplying by \(3\), the same order, spread out. After multiplying by \(-3\), the order is exactly reversed.

In every case, two different numbers end up in two different places. Nothing lands on top of anything else. (The one exception is multiplying by \(0\), which squashes everything onto \(0\). That is why \(ax+b=c\) needs \(a\neq0\).)

An equation asks which inputs land on a given output. If an operation never puts two numbers in the same place, at most one input can land there. A linear equation like \(4x+3=11\) is built from one stretch and one slide, so it has one solution.

Adding an unknown to itself doesn't change this. \(x+x\) is \(2x\): a stretch by the known number \(2\).

Multiplying an Unknown by Itself

\(x\cdot x\) is different in kind. The multiplier is no longer a fixed number: it is \(x\) itself, so it changes from one input to the next.

  • A positive number is multiplied by a positive number. It is stretched and stays on the right: \(3\cdot3=9\).
  • A negative number is multiplied by a negative number. It is stretched and flipped to the right: \((-3)(-3)=9\).

\(3\) and \(-3\) are the same distance from \(0\), so they are stretched by the same amount. One is flipped and the other isn't, but the flipped one started on the left. Both land on \(9\).

So \(x\cdot x\) does two things. It folds the left half of the number line over onto the right half, so \(-3\) lands on \(3\). Then it stretches each number by its own size, so \(3\) becomes \(9\).

The fold is at \(0\) because that is where the multiplier changes sign. Every input left of \(0\) is multiplied by a negative number and flipped; every input right of \(0\) isn't.

What the Fold Does

Two things follow from the fold, and neither can happen with \(x+x\).

Bigger inputs can give smaller outputs. With \(x+x\), a bigger input always gives a bigger output. With \(x\cdot x\), going from \(-4\) to \(-3\) drops the output from \(16\) to \(9\).

After the fold, only distance from \(0\) matters, and \(-3\) is closer to \(0\) than \(-4\). Past \(0\), moving right takes inputs further away, so the output grows. So \(x\cdot x\) falls until \(0\) and rises after it: \(0\) is its turning point.

Two inputs share each positive output. For \(x\cdot x=9\), both \(3\) and \(-3\) land on \(9\). These are separate tests: each uses one number throughout, and each passes. (The Multiple Solutions pages start from exactly this worry.) Every positive output gets two inputs, the same distance either side of \(0\). Zero gets one. Negative outputs get none.

That is the bottom-up reason a quadratic can have two solutions: multiplying the unknown by itself folds the number line, and a fold puts two inputs in the same place.

(A check on the idea: \(x\cdot x\cdot x\) flips a negative number twice, which puts it back on the left. So there is no fold, the order is kept, and \(x^3=8\) has only one real solution, \(2\).)

Mixing the Two

Most quadratics mix both operations, like \(x^2+2x\). Two calculations happen to the same \(x\):

  • \(x\cdot x\) gives the same output for \(3\) and \(-3\): \(9\).
  • \(2x\) does not: \(6\) and \(-6\).

Added together, \(3\) gives \(15\) but \(-3\) gives \(3\). The outputs are no longer symmetric about \(0\).

But the fold hasn't gone. \(x^2+2x\) still falls and then rises, so its outputs still come in pairs, around a different point. Axis of Symmetry finds where, and why.

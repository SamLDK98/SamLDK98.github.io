Polynomials

Addition and Multiplication of Unknowns

Adding To All Numbers

Imagine performing addition on every number on the number line at once.

Every number moves in the same direction and the same distance.

Multiplying All Numbers by a Constant

Multiplying by a positive causes every number to spread out from each other. Specifically, each number moves \(x\) times its distance from \(0\).

Multiplying by a negative causes the same movement except every number swaps with its additive inverse counterpart before stretching outwards.

Multiplying All Numbers by Themselves

A negative times a negative is a positive.

All the negatives first turn \(180^\circ\) and land on their positive counterparts.

They then move a proportional distance relative to their own value.

Why Is \(f(x)=x^2\) Symmetric?

Opposite inputs produce identical outputs:

\[f(3)=f(-3)=9\qquad f(2)=f(-2)=4\]

So the left half of the graph is the mirror image of the right half. The mirror is the vertical line \(x=0\), the axis of symmetry.

Zero is the only number that is its own opposite, so its output, \(0\), comes from just one input.

The point \((0,0)\) is the turning point (also called the vertex).

Adding a Linear Term

Now add a linear term:

\[g(x)=x^2+2x\]

The \(x^2\) term treats \(x\) and \(-x\) identically. The \(2x\) term does not: it adds to positive inputs and takes away from negative ones.

The outputs are no longer symmetric around \(0\): \(g(1)=3\), but \(g(-1)=-1\).

But they still pair up. The output \(0\) appears twice, at \(-2\) and \(0\). So does \(3\), at \(-3\) and \(1\), and \(8\), at \(-4\) and \(2\). Each pair sits the same distance either side of \(-1\), and \(-1\) is the only input whose output appears once.

So the axis of symmetry has moved to \(x=-1\), and the turning point with it. Why there?

Why the Turning Point Moves

Think about what happens when \(x\) increases by \(1\).

For \(x^2\), the change depends on where we are. For \(2x\), it is always \(+2\).

On its own, \(x^2\) falls until \(0\). Its falls shrink as it gets there: \(5\), then \(3\), then \(1\).

The \(2x\) term adds a steady \(2\) on every step. Once \(x^2\) is falling by less than \(2\), the total starts rising.

That happens before \(x^2\) reaches \(0\), so the turning point moves left.

Why exactly to \(-1\)? The changes in \(x^2\) are the odd numbers: \(-5,-3,-1,+1,+3\), switching from falling to rising at \(0\). Adding \(2\) to each gives \(-3,-1,+1,+3,+5\): the same numbers, one step to the left. So the switch comes one step earlier, at \(-1\).

Read the total change column again: the graph falls by \(3\), then \(1\), and rises by \(1\), then \(3\). The climb after \(-1\) is the descent before it, in reverse. That is the symmetry again.

Where Any Quadratic Turns

Take any quadratic of the form

\[f(x)=x^2+bx\]

Increase \(x\) by a very small amount \(h\). The change in the function is:

\[\begin{aligned} f(x+h)-f(x)&=(x+h)^2+b(x+h)-(x^2+bx)\\ &=2xh+h^2+bh\\ &=h(2x+b+h) \end{aligned}\]

\(h\) is positive, so the change has the same sign as \(2x+b+h\). When \(h\) is tiny, that is the sign of \(2x+b\).

So \(f\) falls while \(2x+b\) is negative, and rises once it is positive. It turns where:

\[2x+b=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad x=-\frac{b}{2}\]

For \(g\), \(b=2\), so it turns at \(x=-1\).

The larger \(b\), the further left the turning point. A negative \(b\) puts it to the right of \(0\): \(x^2-5x\) turns at \(2.5\), the mirror line on Multiple Solutions (1).

Why the Two Sides Match

Knowing where the graph turns doesn't yet explain why its two sides are mirror images.

Measure each input from the turning point instead: write \(x=-\frac{b}{2}+k\), where \(k\) is how far \(x\) is from the turning point. Then:

\[\begin{aligned} f\left(-\tfrac{b}{2}+k\right)&=\left(-\tfrac{b}{2}+k\right)^2+b\left(-\tfrac{b}{2}+k\right)\\ &=\tfrac{b^2}{4}-bk+k^2-\tfrac{b^2}{2}+bk\\ &=k^2-\tfrac{b^2}{4} \end{aligned}\]

The output depends only on \(k^2\). Steps of \(k\) and \(-k\) give the same \(k^2\), so inputs the same distance either side of \(-\frac{b}{2}\) give the same output, just as opposite inputs did for \(x^2\).

For \(g\): \(g(-1+k)=k^2-1\). Measured from anywhere else, a \(k\) term is left over, and opposite steps give different outputs. Only from the turning point does it vanish.

Leading Coefficients and Constants

For \(f(x)=ax^2+bx+c\), the same small step gives a change of \(h(2ax+b+ah)\), so the turning point is where \(2ax+b=0\):

\[x=-\frac{b}{2a}\]

If \(a\) is positive, \(2ax+b\) climbs from negative to positive as \(x\) grows. The graph falls and then rises, and the turning point is its lowest point.

If \(a\) is negative, \(2ax+b\) drops from positive to negative. The graph rises and then falls, and the turning point is its highest point.

The constant \(c\) adds the same amount to every output. It lifts or lowers the whole graph but never moves it sideways, so the axis stays put.

Mixing the Two

Most quadratics mix both operations, like \(x^2+2x\). Two calculations happen to the same \(x\):

  • \(x\cdot x\) gives the same output for \(3\) and \(-3\): \(9\).
  • \(2x\) does not: \(6\) and \(-6\).

Added together, \(3\) gives \(15\) but \(-3\) gives \(3\). The outputs are no longer symmetric about \(0\).

But the fold hasn't gone. \(x^2+2x\) still falls and then rises, so its outputs still come in pairs, around a different point. Following the calculation one step at a time shows where.

Following a Calculation Step by Step

Most expressions are built from several steps, not one. We can follow a handful of numbers through every step, one number line at a time. Each number keeps its colour, and a thread joins it from one line to the next.

In \(4x+3\), the numbers are stretched, then slid. Neither step lets two numbers meet, so seven inputs give seven different outputs, and \(4x+3=11\) has one solution.

In \(-2x+5\), the stretch is also a flip. The threads all cross and the order is reversed, but still nothing meets.

In \(x\cdot x\), the threads from opposite numbers meet in pairs.

\(x^2+2x\) can be built in three steps: slide by \(1\), multiply by itself, then slide back.

\[(x+1)^2-1=x^2+2x+1-1=x^2+2x\]

The squaring step still folds at \(0\), but the numbers that are at \(0\) when they reach it started at \(x=-1\). So the pairs meet around \(-1\) instead.

\(x\cdot x\cdot x\) folds and then unfolds. Its last step multiplies the negative numbers by a negative again, which sends them back to the left. Nothing ends up together, which is why \(x^3=8\) has only one real solution, \(2\).

Axis of Symmetry looks more closely at why the fold moves to \(-1\), and finds where it sits for any quadratic.

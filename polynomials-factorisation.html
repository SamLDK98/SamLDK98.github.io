Polynomials

Factorisation

We have seen that we can expand the product of binomials to construct a sum of powers.

Factorisation is the reverse process.

Factorisation Techniques

The Sum-and-Product Method

Imagine:

\[x^2+7x+12=0\]

We know that \((x+p)(x+q)=x^2+(p+q)x+pq\), so we need:

\[\begin{aligned}p+q&=7\\ p\cdot q&=12\end{aligned}\]

We could list the factors of \(12\) and see which could be added to give \(7\).

Factors of 12Their Sum
12 and 113
6 and 28
4 and 37

Therefore:

\[\boxed{x^2+7x+12=(x+3)(x+4)}\]

If we set the quadratic equal to zero, we can immediately determine its roots:

\[(x+3)(x+4)=0\]
\[\boxed{x=-3 \qquad \text{or} \qquad x=-4}\]

The AC Method

Imagine:

\[2x^2+7x+3=0\]

When the coefficient of the squared term is greater than \(1\), we need a variation on the previous technique.

Find two numbers whose:

  • Sum is \(b\)
  • Product is \(ac\)
\[a=2, \qquad b=7, \qquad c=3\]
  • Sum needed: \(7\)
  • Product needed: \(2 \cdot 3 = 6\)

\(6\) and \(1\) will work.

We split the middle term.

\[2x^2+6x+x+3\]

Group the terms into pairs:

\[(2x^2 + x) + (6x + 3)\]

Factor out the greatest common factor (GCF) from each pair:

\[(2x^2 + x) \;\rightarrow\; x(2x + 1)\]
\[(6x + 3) \;\rightarrow\; 3(2x + 1)\]

Combine them back together:

\[x(2x + 1) + 3(2x + 1)\]

Factor out the common binomial factor \((2x + 1)\):

\[(2x + 1)(x + 3)\]

A Non-Monic Quadratic with a GCF

However, if we have a non-monic quadratic that has a greatest common factor:

\[6x^2 -18x +12\]

We always want the leading coefficient to be \(1\).

We don't actually divide the expression by \(6\). We factor it out to give:

\[(6)(x^2-3x+2)\]

We then factor the quadratic expression if it fits into one of the forms shown above.

\[(6)(x-1)(x-2)\]

The zero-product property still demands either \(x-1\) or \(x-2\) be equal to zero for this product to be equal to zero.

Special Binomial Identities

Square of a Sum

\[\boxed{(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2}\]

Look out for:

  • Is the constant a perfect square?
  • Is the middle term positive and twice the product of the square roots of the first and last terms?

Example:

\[x^2 + 6x + 9\]

The middle term is \(6x\).

1st term (\(x^2\)) square root is \(x\).

Last term (\(9\)) square root is \(3\).

\[2 \cdot 3 \cdot x = 6x\]

So we know that this is a perfect square trinomial.

Matching this to our identity where \(a = x\) and \(b=3\), the factors take the form \((a+b)(a+b)\); in other words \((a+b)^2\).

\[x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2\]

We have recovered a single repeated factor.

If this expression is set equal to zero:

\[(x+3)^2=0\]

Then:

\[\boxed{x=-3}\]

The factor \(x+3\) occurs twice, so we call \(-3\) a repeated root, or a root of multiplicity two.

Square of a Difference

\[\boxed{(a-b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2}\]

Look out for:

  • Is the constant a perfect square?
  • Is the middle term negative?
  • Is the middle term negative twice the product of the square roots of the first and last terms?

Example:

\[x^2-8x+16\]

The middle term is \(-8x\).

1st term (\(x^2\)) square root is \(x\).

Last term (\(16\)) square root is \(4\).

\[-2\cdot4\cdot x=-8x\]

So we know that this is a perfect square trinomial.

Matching this to our identity where \(a=x\) and \(b=4\), the factors take the form \((a-b)(a-b)\); in other words, \((a-b)^2\).

\[x^2-8x+16=(x-4)^2\]

We have recovered a single repeated factor.

If this expression is set equal to zero:

\[(x-4)^2=0\]

Then:

\[\boxed{x=4}\]

The factor \(x-4\) occurs twice, so we call \(4\) a repeated root, or a root of multiplicity two.

Difference of Two Squares

\[\boxed{(a+b)(a-b) = a^2 - b^2}\]

This occurs when the middle terms cancel during expansion.

Look out for:

  • No linear term.
  • Separated by a minus.
  • The constant is a perfect square.

We just need to find the square root of the constant.

For example:

\[x^2 - 25 = (x + 5)(x - 5)\]

While the square of a sum and the square of a difference both produce perfect square trinomials, the product of a sum and a difference produces a difference of two squares.

These Methods Do Not Always Work

We may come across quadratics where no pair of integers satisfies the sum-product constraints.

In such cases, we can use a more robust method: completing the square.

Factors

The factors of a value are the whole numbers that can be multiplied by another whole number to give that value.

\(1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\) are all factors of \(12\).

Notice that they are also the numbers that \(12\) is divisible by (that gives whole numbers).

Prime factorisation reveals the multiplicative structure of a number.

For example, \(60\) could be expressed as: \(2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5\).

A polynomial can be a factor of another polynomial.

\[(a + b)(c +d) = e\]

Both polynomials on the LHS are factors of \(e\).

Consider the binomial \(4x+18\).

\(2\) is the largest number that both \(4\) and \(18\) are divisible by: the greatest common factor.

So \((2)\) and \((2x+9)\) are both factors. Not only are they both factors, but they are the irreducible factors. The coefficients \(2\) and \(9\) share no common factor other than \(1\).

Consider:

\[x^3-6x^2+11x-6\]

Factored form:

\[(x-1)(x-2)(x-3)\]

Just as integers can be decomposed into prime factors, polynomials can be decomposed into irreducible factors: polynomial factors that cannot be broken down further over the coefficient system we're using.

So in a very real sense, finding all the roots of a polynomial is equivalent to finding its complete factorisation into linear factors.

Factor Theorem

When we know the irreducible factors of a polynomial, roots are trivial to identify.

Suppose we want to solve:

\[(x+2)(x+3)=0\]

We know that a product of two real numbers can equal zero only if at least one factor equals zero.

Therefore \(x = -2\) and \(x = -3\) are both solutions.

Likewise, if we know that \(r\) is a root of a quadratic, it means \((x - r)\) must be a factor.

This is called the factor theorem.

Formally (where \(Q(x)\) is another polynomial):

\[P(r)=0\iff P(x)=(x-r)Q(x)\]

Polynomials

Multiple Solutions

One Number at a Time

Here is a quadratic equation:

\[x^2-5x=-6\]

It has two solutions: \(2\) and \(3\).

That can feel wrong. There is only one unknown. Shouldn't there be only one answer?

Look at what happens when we test a number. Whatever number we try, every \(x\) in the equation takes that same number:

\[\begin{aligned} 2^2-5(2)&=4-10=-6\\ 3^2-5(3)&=9-15=-6 \end{aligned}\]

\(x\) is never \(2\) in one place and \(3\) in the other.

An equation is a test. A number either makes it true or it doesn't. Solving means finding every number that passes.

Here, two different numbers pass, each on its own:

\[x=2\quad\text{or}\quad x=3\]

So the real question is: how can a test with one unknown let two numbers through?

Why We Expect One Answer

Take a linear equation:

\[x+x=10\]

As \(x\) increases, \(x+x\) only ever increases. It reaches \(10\) once, at \(x=5\), and then climbs past it.

Once it has passed \(10\), it never comes back. So only one number can pass the test.

It isn't about how many times \(x\) appears. \(x+x=10\) contains two \(x\)'s and has one solution. \(x^2=9\) contains one \(x\) and has two: \(3\) and \(-3\).

What matters is whether the left-hand side can turn around.

Two Processes, One Unknown

In \(x^2-5x\), the same \(x\) goes through two processes. It is squared, and it is multiplied by \(5\). Then one result is taken from the other.

The two processes grow at different speeds. Moving the terms across, the equation becomes \(5x-x^2=6\): it asks when \(5x\) is exactly \(6\) ahead of \(x^2\). That lead is the amber gap.

Each time \(x\) goes up by \(1\), \(5x\) goes up by \(5\). Always \(5\). But \(x^2\) goes up by more each time:

\(x\)\(0\to1\)\(1\to2\)\(2\to3\)\(3\to4\)\(4\to5\)
\(x^2\) grows by\(1\)\(3\)\(5\)\(7\)\(9\)
\(5x\) grows by\(5\)\(5\)\(5\)\(5\)\(5\)
\(x^2-5x\) changes by\(-4\)\(-2\)\(0\)\(+2\)\(+4\)

At first \(5x\) grows faster, so \(x^2-5x\) falls. Then \(x^2\) catches up and overtakes, and \(x^2-5x\) rises again.

The step from \(2\) to \(3\) is a tie: both grow by \(5\). So \(x^2-5x\) ends that step exactly where it started. That is why \(2\) and \(3\) give the same result.

Falling, then rising. Anything that falls and then rises can pass the same level twice: once on the way down, once on the way up.

\(x^2-5x\) passes \(-6\) at \(x=2\) on the way down, and again at \(x=3\) on the way up.

Its lowest point is \(-6.25\), at \(x=2.5\). Ask for a level lower than that, and no number passes at all.

The Two Answers Are Partners

The lead of \(5x\) over \(x^2\) can also be drawn as an area.

Picture \(5x\) as a strip, \(x\) tall and \(5\) wide, and \(x^2\) as a square, \(x\) by \(x\). Set the square inside the strip. What is left over is \(x\) tall and \(5-x\) wide, and its area is \(5x-x^2\).

As \(x\) grows, the leftover gets taller but narrower. Its area rises, then falls.

At \(x=2\) it is \(2\) tall and \(3\) wide. At \(x=3\) it is \(3\) tall and \(2\) wide. It is the same rectangle stood on its side, and both have area \(6\).

So the two solutions come as a pair: swap the height and the width, and one becomes the other.

Where Factoring Fits

Moving everything to one side gives \(x^2-5x+6=0\). Written as \((x-2)(x-3)=0\), the equation shows where its two crossings are. That is the product form doing its job. The rise and fall is why there are two.

Higher powers can turn around more often. A cubic can fall, rise and fall again, so it can pass the same level three times.

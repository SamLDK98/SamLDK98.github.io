The Origins of Counting

Sixty thousand years separate the first notch cut into a bone from the first written numeral. Almost all of that time was spent on one hard idea: that how many can be prised loose from how many of what.

Before numberOne thing for one thing

You can keep an exact count without having a single number word. Cut one notch for each hide, and the bone holds the tally whether or not you can say "seventeen". Drop one pebble into a pouch for each sheep that leaves in the morning, take one out for each that returns, and an empty pouch means the flock is whole. This is one-to-one correspondence, and it is the deepest layer of counting — older than arithmetic, older than any symbol.

What survives of it is what was cut into something hard. The archaeological record of counting is therefore a record of three technologies in turn: notches in bone, tokens in clay, and finally marks that stand for the tokens.

Phase oneNotches, 60,000 – 20,000 BCE

The oldest candidates are marks on bone. A hyena leg bone from Les Pradelles in France, carved by Neanderthals around 60,000 BCE, carries nine notches close enough in form to suggest one hand returning to the same task. The Lebombo bone, a baboon fibula carrying 29 notches, found on the Eswatini/South Africa border, is old enough — c. 42,000 BCE — and regular enough to have been read as a lunar tally.

Then something changes. The Vestonice wolf bone from around 30,000 BCE carries 55 notches arranged in groups of five, and the Ishango bone from c. 20,000 BCE carries three separate columns of grouped marks, with totals and runs that people have been arguing about ever since. Whatever those columns mean, grouping is not tallying. A tally accumulates; a group is a structure someone has imposed on the count so it can be read at a glance. That is the germ of a base.

A caution worth keeping

Notches are ambiguous evidence. Cut marks can come from butchery, from decoration, from idle work. The case for counting rests on regularity, on repetition over time, and on grouping — which is why the Ishango readings remain contested while the fact of its deliberate grouping does not.

Phase twoTokens, 8,000 – 3,500 BCE

Farming broke tallying. A notch says "how many" but not "of what", and a settled economy has to track grain and oil and sheep at once, across seasons, between people who may not trust each other. The answer, found across the Fertile Crescent from around 8,000 BCE, was to make the count out of clay: small geometric tokens, a cone for one measure of grain, a sphere for a larger unit, a disk for something else again.

A token is still one-to-one — seven sheep is seven tokens — but the shape now carries the commodity, and the count is portable, storable and auditable. Around 4,000 BCE Sumerian traders began sealing a transaction's tokens inside a hollow clay envelope, the bulla, so nobody could quietly add or remove one in transit.

That created an obvious problem: you cannot read a sealed envelope without destroying it. The fix was to press each token into the soft outer clay before enclosing it. For the first time the record on the outside was a mark standing for an object — and the mark turned out to be the useful half.

Phase threeNumerals, 3,300 – 1,200 BCE

At Uruk, around 3,300 BCE, scribes stopped sealing the tokens at all and impressed the shapes straight onto flat tablets. These proto-cuneiform tablets are the first formal written counting system, and they contain the decisive move: a sign for the quantity, written beside a sign for the commodity. Five sheep is no longer a picture of five sheep. It is five, which applies equally to sheep, to jars and to days.

After that, the answers multiply. Egypt, by around 3,000 BCE, had a formal base-10 system with one sign per power of ten: a stroke for 1, a heel bone for 10, a coiled rope for 100, a lotus for 1,000. The Indus Valley by 2,000 BCE was running standardised weights in binary and decimal ratios through its trade and taxation. And the Shang oracle bones of the Yellow River basin, around 1,200 BCE, carry a fully developed decimal system building large numbers from combined glyphs.

Three civilisations, largely independent, all landing on ten. The reason is almost certainly attached to the ends of your arms.

The shape of the arcWhat actually got invented

Read as a whole, the sequence is not a story of people slowly learning to count. People could always count. It is a story of the count being progressively detached — first from the thing being counted, then from the counter, and finally from the physical object doing the counting.

  • A notch is tied to the event that made it.
  • A token is tied to a commodity but survives the event.
  • An impression is tied to a token, but is only a mark.
  • A numeral is tied to nothing at all — and that is what makes it arithmetic.

Everything that follows in this section — place value, zero, negative numbers, the irrationals — is a further round of the same move, and each one was resisted for exactly the same reason: it asks you to treat as a number something you cannot point at.

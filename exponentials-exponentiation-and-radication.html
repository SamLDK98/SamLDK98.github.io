Exponentials

A Deeper Understanding of Exponentiation and Radication

To gain a clear understanding of fractional exponents, we need to deepen our understanding of exponentiation and radication.

First we need to understand that division breaks numbers down into additive parts, while radication breaks numbers down into multiplicative parts.

We will then consider how the journey of moving from one value to another differs when the steps are additive and when they are multiplicative.

Finally, we will consider exponents and radicals in light of what we have discussed.

Equal Parts and Equal Factors

Division: equal parts that add

Division decomposes a number into a sum of identical values.

The number of values in that sum is given by the number we are dividing by.

Take \(64\). We want two equal numbers that, when added, give it.

\[x+x=64\]

We divide the number by \(2\), of course.

\[2x=64 \qquad x=\frac{64}{2}=32\]

If we were to divide by \(4\), the result would be the value of which four copies add up to the original number.

But what if we want the value of which a given number of copies, multiplied together, make the original number?

Radication: equal factors that multiply

Radication decomposes a number into a product of identical values.

The number of factors in that product is given by the root's index: the small number in front of the root sign, such as the \(3\) in \(\sqrt[3]{64}\).

Imagine we want to decompose \(64\) into two identical values that, when multiplied, give it.

\[x\cdot x=64 \qquad x^2=64\]

Taking the square root gives this value:

\[x=\sqrt{64}=8\]

Just as division finds the value of equal additive parts, radication finds the value of equal multiplicative factors.

Seeing the Ratio

What does it mean to grow by the same factor at every step? Start with a square of area \(1\). Imagine a \(2\times2\) frame, with each of its four spaces large enough to hold a copy of that square.

Place one copy in each space, then let the frame disappear. The four copies form a larger square. Repeat, using the whole new square as the piece you copy.

Each new square has four times the area of the previous one. Both side lengths double, so the area is multiplied by \(2\times2=4\). The amount of area added changes, but the ratio stays the same.

Now start with the square of area \(256\), and reverse the process. Halve both side lengths and keep one of the four equal squares. Repeat with the square that remains.

Each remaining square occupies the same proportion of the previous square: one quarter. After two of the four reductions, the area is \(16\). We have completed half the steps, although the area is far less than half of \(256\).

Taking the fourth root asks: what positive factor, used four times starting from \(1\), gives \(256\)? The enlargements show the answer:

\[\underbrace{4\times4\times4\times4}_{\text{four equal factors}}=256\qquad\sqrt[4]{256}=4\]

The root here is the factor by which the area grows at each step. The side lengths grow by a factor of \(2\).

Additive and Multiplicative Change

Let's say we start at \(1\) and move towards \(100\).

If we move by the same amount at each step, that is additive change.

Equal distances along the number line then take the same number of steps. For example, moving from \(20\) to \(40\) takes as many steps as moving from \(80\) to \(100\).

If we move by a constant multiplication factor instead, that is multiplicative change.

As we move from left to right, the distance covered by each step increases (assuming our multiplication factor is greater than \(1\)).

This means that moving from \(20\) to \(40\) takes more multiplicative steps than moving from \(80\) to \(100\).

Halfway

The key realisation is that the halfway point between two numbers in terms of addition is simply the halfway value between them. Between \(25\) and \(125\), it is \(75\).

The halfway point in terms of multiplication, however, is less straightforward.

Halfway means the two halves of the journey are equal steps. By adding, the two halves add the same amount. By multiplying, they multiply by the same factor.

Move the middle point until the two halves are equal. There are two such places.

By adding, halfway is \(75\): \(+50\) and then \(+50\). By multiplying, halfway is about \(55.90\): \(\times2.236\) and then \(\times2.236\) again.

And \(55.90\) is \(5^{2.5}\), the middle row of the table above. Halfway in the exponent is halfway by multiplying.

Exponents and Radicals

An exponent counts multiplicative steps. Starting from \(1\), \(5^3\) takes three steps of \(\times5\):

\[1 \xrightarrow{\times5} 5 \xrightarrow{\times5} 25 \xrightarrow{\times5} 125\]

\(5^0=1\) is the start, with no steps taken. Adding \(1\) to the exponent takes one more step, which is why adding to the exponent multiplies the value.

So what is a fraction of a step?

Half a step of \(\times5\) is a factor that, taken twice, makes \(\times5\). Finding it means splitting \(5\) into two equal factors: radication.

\[5^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot5^{\frac{1}{2}}=5^1 \qquad 5^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{5}\approx2.236\]

It is the factor we found at halfway. Two whole steps and a half step from \(1\) land on \(55.90\):

\[5^{2.5}=5^2\cdot5^{\frac{1}{2}}=25\sqrt{5}\approx55.90\]

In general, for \(a>0\), \(\frac{1}{n}\) of a step of \(\times a\) is one of the \(n\) equal factors of \(a\), and \(m\) such pieces are \(m\) of them:

\[a^{\frac{1}{n}}=\sqrt[n]{a} \qquad \boxed{a^{\frac{m}{n}}=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m}\]

This is the multiplicative twin of taking a fraction of a number. Two of three equal parts of \(64\) are \(\frac{2}{3}\times64\approx42.67\). Two of three equal factors are \(\left(\sqrt[3]{64}\right)^2=4^2=16\).

Radication and exponentiation undo each other. Multiplying the \(n\) equal factors of \(a\) back together gives \(a\) again:

\[\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^n=a^{\frac{n}{n}}=a\]

One question is left: how many steps of \(\times5\) make \(125\)? Counting steps is the job of the logarithm, for a later page.

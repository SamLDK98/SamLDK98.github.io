Polynomials

Why Only Like Terms Can Be Combined

Like Terms

Coefficients are multipliers of terms.

When two terms differ only by their coefficients, we can add the coefficients and multiply the term by this sum.

Example:

\[2x+3x=5x\]

This is just the Both terms share the factor \(x\). Take it out, and add the coefficients to get the number that multiplies it: \[ax+bx=x(a+b)\] Read backwards, it is the distributive property: \(x(a+b)=ax+bx\). in action.

Coefficients denote a number of copies of a term.

\[2x\rightarrow x+x\qquad 3x\rightarrow x+x+x\]

Two copies of \(x\) and three copies of \(x\) are the same as five copies of \(x\):

\[(x+x)+(x+x+x)=x+x+x+x+x\]

The term being counted stays the same. Only the number of copies changes. That's why we only need to change the coefficient.

Unlike Terms

\[2a\rightarrow a+a\qquad 3b\rightarrow b+b+b\]

Two copies of \(a\) and three copies of \(b\) are:

  • not five copies of \(a\),
  • not five copies of \(b\),
  • and not five copies of \((a+b)\), because \(5(a+b)\) would be five copies of \(a\) and five copies of \(b\).

But Is the Unknown Not the Same Quantity in \(x\) and \(x^2\)?!

The unknown is the same quantity, but the term multiplied by the coefficient is not.

\[\begin{aligned} 3x\ &\rightarrow\ \text{three copies of the value that is } x\\ 3x^2\ &\rightarrow\ \text{three copies of the value that is } (x\cdot x) \end{aligned}\]

If \(x=5\):

  • \(3x\rightarrow5+5+5\)
  • \(3x^2\rightarrow25+25+25\)

Just like \(a\) and \(b\) represent different quantities, so do \(x\) and \(x^2\).

To be extra explicit:

\[2x^2+3x^2=5x^2\]
\[1\text{ copy}\rightarrow(x\cdot x)\]

So:

\[\begin{aligned} &2(x\cdot x)+3(x\cdot x)=5(x\cdot x)\\ &2x+2x+3x+3x=5x+5x\\ &(x+x)+(x+x)+(x+x+x)+(x+x+x)=(x+x+x+x+x)+(x+x+x+x+x) \end{aligned}\]

Key Takeaway

Each unique power of \(x\) refers to a different quantity.

\[x^2,\ x^3,\ x^4\]

These are all unlike terms.

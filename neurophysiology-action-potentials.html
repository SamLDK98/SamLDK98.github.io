Action Potentials

One spike, taken apart. Not the equations that generate it — those are chapter 01 — but the membrane itself, moment by moment: which channels are open, which are plugged, and which way the ions are actually moving.

The remitA diagram that cannot lie

Almost every action potential diagram you have seen was drawn by hand. Someone decided where to put the gates, and the picture is only as accurate as their memory of the physiology. The usual result is a cartoon that is roughly right about the shape and quietly wrong about the mechanism.

The model below is drawn from the data. A single Hodgkin–Huxley spike is integrated when the page loads, and every gate in the illustration is positioned by reading m, h and n out of that solution at the instant you are looking at. Drag the playhead and the channels move because the numbers moved. Nothing in the picture is drawn from memory.

The phase boundaries are derived too. Threshold, the peak, the conductance crossover and the trough of the undershoot are found by searching the computed trace, not typed in as timestamps — so the coloured bands cannot drift out of step with the curve they are labelling.

The five phasesWhat happens, and when

These times come from the integration, for a 0.5 ms, 20 µA/cm² stimulus delivered at 1 ms into a squid axon membrane at 6.3 °C.

PhaseTimeWhat the membrane is doing
Resting 0 – 1.0 ms Sodium shut but ready (m 0.05, h 0.60); potassium partly open. The three currents cancel.
To threshold 1.0 – 1.92 ms Injected charge lifts the membrane. Nothing regenerative yet — remove the stimulus here and it simply decays.
Depolarising 1.92 – 3.12 ms Runaway. m opens, sodium enters, which opens more m. Peak rate of rise 298 mV/ms.
Repolarising 3.12 – 5.30 ms Sodium inactivates as potassium finally opens. The two conductances cross at 4.28 ms.
Undershoot 5.30 – 17.4 ms Potassium is slow to shut, so it overshoots rest, bottoming at −76.2 mV before drifting back.

The correctionThe peak is not where sodium stops

Here is the thing the cartoons get wrong, and it is worth scrubbing to the peak to watch it happen.

The standard story says the spike turns over because the sodium channels close. They do not. At the peak, the sodium conductance is still rising — it does not reach its own maximum until 3.24 ms, a tenth of a millisecond after the voltage has already turned over, and m keeps climbing until 3.64 ms.

At the peak — 3.12 ms, +39.6 mV

INa = −306  ·  IK = +276  ·  Ileak = +28  µA/cm²  →  net ≈ 0

The peak is simply where the currents balance. And the reason sodium current falls while sodium conductance is still climbing is the driving force: at +39.6 mV the membrane is only 10 mV away from sodium's own equilibrium potential, so each open channel carries almost nothing. Sodium does not switch off at the top of the spike. It runs out of somewhere to push.

Two gatesWhy one channel needs two independent parts

The sodium channel in the illustration has two moving pieces, and keeping them separate is the whole point. The activation gate follows m and opens fast. The inactivation ball follows h, is tethered inside the cell, and swings up to plug the pore — slowly, and in response to the same depolarisation that opened the gate.

Scrub to the undershoot and look at the sodium channel. Its activation gate is still open. The pore is blocked anyway, by the ball. That combination — open but plugged — is the refractory period, and it is why a diagram that simply closes the gate teaches the wrong thing. The channel cannot conduct again until the ball withdraws, and the ball is slow.

The potassium channel, by contrast, has no inactivation at all. It is a delayed rectifier: slow to open, slow to close, and nothing plugs it. That is why it is still wide open at 5.24 ms — gK = 11.0 against gNa = 0.5 — dragging the membrane past rest into the undershoot.

HonestyWhere the picture stops being literal

Three things the illustration does that the physiology does not, worth knowing before you trust it too far:

  • It draws one channel of each kind, but m, h and n are population fractions. When h reads 0.34, that is not one ball two-thirds of the way in — it is roughly a third of the channels not yet inactivated. A single drawn channel cannot honestly show a probability, so it shows the average instead.
  • The gates are a description, not a mechanism. Hodgkin and Huxley invented m, h and n to fit their data thirty years before anyone saw a channel. The ball-and-chain drawn here is the structure later found to do the job, but the equations never required it to look like that.
  • Nothing is to scale. The membrane is about 5 nm thick and the channels are a few nanometres across; the ion counts are illustrative, and real fluxes move a vanishingly small fraction of the ions present. A spike changes the internal sodium concentration by roughly one part in a hundred thousand.

What is not exaggerated is the timing, the voltages, the conductances and the gate values. Those are the integration, and you can check any of them against the readout at the top of the figure.

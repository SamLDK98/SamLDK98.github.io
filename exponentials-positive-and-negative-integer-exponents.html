Exponentials

Integer Exponents

The power \(3^4\) denotes the product of four copies of three:

\[3\cdot3\cdot3\cdot3\]

Positive Integer Exponents

We could start by thinking of exponents as denoting how many copies of the base must appear as factors in a product.

\(4^6\) means six copies of four as factors in a product:

\[4\cdot4\cdot4\cdot4\cdot4\cdot4\]

Nice.

The Zero Exponent

But what does this mean for \(0\) as an exponent?

It gets a little confusing to think in terms of a natural language definition. Instead we can observe the patterns in successive powers to work out how the operation should behave in less intuitive situations.

PowerValue
\(4^0\)?
\(4^1\)\(4\)
\(4^2\)\(16\)
\(4^3\)\(64\)
\(4^4\)\(256\)
\(4^5\)\(1{,}024\)
  • Increasing the exponent multiplies the previous power's value by \(4\).
  • Decreasing the exponent divides the previous power's value by \(4\).

To find \(4^0\) we just divide \(4\) by \(4\) to get \(1\).

In general, any nonzero number raised to the power of zero equals \(1\).

\[\boxed{a^0=1 \qquad (a \ne 0)}\]

Negative Integer Exponents

If we continue dividing the preceding value by the base, negative exponents represent the reciprocal of the base raised to the positive power.

PowerValue
\(4^{5}\)\(1{,}024\)
\(4^{4}\)\(256\)
\(4^{3}\)\(64\)
\(4^{2}\)\(16\)
\(4^{1}\)\(4\)
\(4^{0}\)\(1\)
\(4^{-1}\)\(\frac{1}{4}\)
\(4^{-2}\)\(\frac{1}{16}\)
\(4^{-3}\)\(\frac{1}{64}\)
\(4^{-4}\)\(\frac{1}{256}\)
\(4^{-5}\)\(\frac{1}{1{,}024}\)

The general identity:

\[\boxed{a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n} \qquad (a \ne 0)}\]

Expanded example:

\[a^{-3} = \frac{1}{a} \times \frac{1}{a} \times \frac{1}{a} = \frac{1}{a^3}\]

Exponentials

Positive and Negative Integer Exponents

Positive Integer Exponents

We could start by thinking of exponents as denoting how many copies of the base to include in a product expression.

\(4^6\) means: take six copies of four as factors in a product expression:

\[4\cdot4\cdot4\cdot4\cdot4\cdot4\]

The Zero Exponent

However, what does this mean for \(0\) as an exponent?

We could say that \(4^0\) means: take no copies of four and put them in a product expression, but that would mean it must equal \(0\).

Any number multiplied by \(0\) equals \(0\), so this might seem to make sense.

But we need \(4^0\) to equal \(1\).

If we observe the following pattern, we see that each time we raise the exponent by \(1\), from \(a^n\) to \(a^{n+1}\), the value of the previous power is multiplied by the base:

\[\begin{array}{ccccc} 4^{\class{mjx-exp}{1}} & \overset{\class{mjx-base}{\times 4}}{\longrightarrow} & 4^{\class{mjx-exp}{2}} & \overset{\class{mjx-base}{\times 4}}{\longrightarrow} & 4^{\class{mjx-exp}{3}} \\[2pt] 4 && 16 && 64 \end{array}\]

In reverse, when we decrease the exponent by \(1\), from \(a^n\) to \(a^{n-1}\), we divide the value of the power by the base:

\[\begin{array}{ccccccc} 4^{\class{mjx-exp}{3}} & \overset{\class{mjx-base}{\div 4}}{\longrightarrow} & 4^{\class{mjx-exp}{2}} & \overset{\class{mjx-base}{\div 4}}{\longrightarrow} & 4^{\class{mjx-exp}{1}} & \overset{\class{mjx-base}{\div 4}}{\longrightarrow} & 4^{\class{mjx-exp}{0}} \\[2pt] 64 && 16 && 4 && ? \end{array}\]

Following from this, decreasing the exponent from \(1\) to \(0\) must result in \(1\), as we would be dividing one copy of the base by itself:

\[4^0=\frac{4}{4}=1\]

This holds for any base \(a\) except \(0\), since we cannot divide by \(0\):

\[\boxed{a^0=1}\]

We could keep trying to think of exponents in natural language; however, the definition becomes more complicated than just pointing to the rules. If you want a cleaner, more complete understanding of why \(x\cdot0=0\) and why \(x^0=1\), you can look into the idea of identity elements. For now, however, we will shift towards thinking about the rules of exponents rather than any geometric or conceptual meaning.

Identity elements

Adding \(0\) changes nothing, and multiplying by \(1\) changes nothing: \(0\) is the identity for addition, and \(1\) the identity for multiplication.

Taking no copies leaves the identity. A sum of no copies of \(x\) is \(0\), so \(x\cdot0=0\). A product of no copies of \(x\) is \(1\), so \(x^0=1\).

\[\boxed{a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}}\]

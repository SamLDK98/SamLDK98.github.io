Where Do Multiple Different Solutions Come From?

Why Do Univariate Equations Typically Have Only One Solution?

Quadratic equations can seem counterintuitive if you're accustomed to equations with one unknown having only one solution.

An equation with two unknowns has infinitely many possible solutions.

\[3x+2y=5\]

We could assign any value to \(x\) and there will still be a corresponding \(y\) value that satisfies the equation.

However, univariate linear equations typically constrain the possible solutions for \(x\) to one.

\[3x-1=5 \qquad x\text{ can only be }2\]

Any equation equating the sum of one or more copies of \(x\) to a constant has exactly one solution.

All the copies of \(x\) grow at the same rate and in the same direction, so they can only pass each constant once.

\[x=9\qquad 2x=9\qquad 3x=9\qquad 4x=9\]

How Can an Equation With One Unknown Have Two Non-Opposite Solutions?

\(x^2\) can have two solutions, but they are always additive inverses: \(x^2=9\) is solved by \(3\) and \(-3\).

\(x^2-5x=6\) has one unknown but two different, non-opposite solutions (\(-1\) and \(6\)).

There are four questions to unpack:

  1. Why does an \(x^2\) term produce symmetry?
  2. Why does this symmetry have its turning point at \((0,0)\)?
  3. Why does adding a constant to the function move it vertically?
  4. How can adding a linear term move the turning point sideways?

The linear term distinguishes between positive and negative inputs, so the function is no longer symmetric about zero. \(x^2\) gives opposite inputs the same output, but a linear term gives them opposite outputs: \(-2x\) is \(-6\) at \(x=3\) and \(+6\) at \(x=-3\). Added together, each opposite pair no longer lands on the same output.

\(x^2\)

Squaring treats positive and negative values identically.

Every output except \(0\) therefore comes from two inputs.

Moving from negative inputs to positive ones, we pass every positive output twice.

As we move from left to right on the number line, the numbers increase, but their size (absolute value) decreases before increasing after 0.

That's because \(-4<-3\), but \(4>3\) (\(-4\) is the smaller number, but has the bigger size).

Squaring ignores the sign, so the outputs decrease as we move toward 0 and then begin increasing again after 0.

This means the turning point of \(f(x)=x^2\) must occur at the \(x\)-coordinate 0.

The position on the \(y\)-axis is determined by the lowest output. Given the smallest square we can make is \(0\), that is where the \(y\)-coordinate must be.

The turning point, or vertex, of \(f(x)=x^2\) occurs at \((0,0)\).

Because opposite inputs give the same output, the symmetry is across the \(y\)-axis (but the axis of symmetry need not only be here!).

\(x^2+4\)

Adding a constant value to every output lifts everything up or down.

It changes the \(y\)-coordinate, but not the \(x\)-coordinate of the lowest output value.

Technically it changes the \(y\)-coordinate, but not the \(x\)-coordinate, of every point on the graph!

So the turning point moves from \((0,0)\) to \((0,4)\), and the axis of symmetry stays at \(x=0\).

\(x^2-2x\)

The reason adding a constant just lifts everything up or down is because it affects the graph uniformly.

It adds 4 to an output whether the output would be \(2\), \(20\), or \(200\).

However, when \(x\) is also involved in another term, it is going to affect the output in proportion to the input's value.

But because the linear term is proportional to the input, its contribution changes at a constant rate.

If we increase the input by 1:

  • \(-2x\) contributes 2 less to the output.
  • \(x^2\) changes by 2 more than it did on the previous step.

Consider what happens when we combine these contributions:

Notice that the total changes still increase by 2 each time. The linear term changes the size of each change, but not the difference between successive changes.

This explains why the parabola retains its shape.

Back to Two Different Solutions

Read down the total change column: \(-5,-3,-1\), then \(+1,+3,+5\).

The outputs fall until \(x=1\), then rise by the same steps in reverse.

So the turning point has moved from \(0\) to \(1\), and the outputs now mirror each other around \(x=1\) instead of around \(0\).

Inputs the same distance either side of \(1\) give the same output: \(x^2-2x\) is \(3\) at both \(x=-1\) and \(x=3\).

So \(x^2-2x=3\) has two different, non-opposite solutions.

\(x^2-5x=6\) works the same way. With \(-5x\), each step's total change is \(5\) less than the change in \(x^2\): \(-2\) from \(1\) to \(2\), \(0\) from \(2\) to \(3\), \(+2\) from \(3\) to \(4\). The outputs at \(2\) and \(3\) are equal, so the turning point sits halfway between, at \(x=2.5\).

The solutions, \(-1\) and \(6\), are \(3.5\) either side of it.

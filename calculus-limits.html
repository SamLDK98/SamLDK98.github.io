Calculus

Limits

Heading Toward a Value

Consider the function:

\[f(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}\]

At \(x=1\), the top is \(0\) and the bottom is \(0\).

\(\frac{0}{0}\) has no value, so \(f(1)\) does not exist.

But we can still ask what happens near \(1\).

An input of \(\class{mjx-in}{0.9}\) gives \(\class{mjx-out}{1.9}\). An input of \(\class{mjx-in}{0.99}\) gives \(\class{mjx-out}{1.99}\). An input of \(\class{mjx-in}{0.999}\) gives \(\class{mjx-out}{1.999}\).

From the other side, \(\class{mjx-in}{1.1}\) gives \(\class{mjx-out}{2.1}\), and \(\class{mjx-in}{1.01}\) gives \(\class{mjx-out}{2.01}\).

As the input closes in on \(1\), the output closes in on \(2\).

The output never reaches \(2\), because the input is never allowed to reach \(1\).

Yet the value it is heading for is perfectly clear.

That value is called the limit.

\[\lim_{x\to \class{mjx-in}{1}} f(x)=\class{mjx-out}{2}\]

We read this as “the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(1\) is \(2\)”.

The Point Itself Doesn't Matter

Factorising the top shows where the hole comes from.

\[\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=\frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1}=x+1 \quad (x\neq1)\]

Everywhere except \(x=1\), \(f\) is simply the line \(x+1\). Only one point is missing.

A limit only looks at inputs near \(1\), never at \(1\) itself.

So if we filled the hole with some other value, say \(f(1)=4\), the limit would still be \(2\).

The function now has a value at \(1\), but everything around it is still heading for \(2\).

When the value at a point does match the limit there, the function is called continuous at that point: no hole, and no stray dot.

For a continuous function, the limit is found just by substituting.

\[\lim_{x\to3}\,(x+1)=3+1=4\]

Polynomials, sine and cosine are continuous everywhere. Limits only become interesting where substituting fails.

Both Sides Must Agree

An input can approach \(1\) from below or from above.

Each direction gives a one-sided limit:

\[\lim_{x\to1^-} f(x)=2 \qquad \lim_{x\to1^+} f(x)=2\]

The minus means from below, and the plus means from above.

The limit exists only when both sides settle on the same number.

Here are three functions where they don't, each approached at \(x=0\).

In the jump, each side settles, but on a different number: \(1\) from below, \(2\) from above.

In the blow-up, the outputs grow without end. There is no number to settle on.

In the wobble, \(\sin\frac{1}{x}\), the outputs keep swinging between \(-1\) and \(1\), however close the input gets.

None of these has a limit at \(0\).

As Close as We Like

So far, “heading for” has been doing a lot of work.

\(1.9,\ 1.99,\ 1.999\) are getting closer to \(2\). But they are also getting closer to \(3\), and to \(100\).

Getting closer is not enough. What makes \(2\) special is that the outputs get as close to \(2\) as we like.

This matters most when there is nothing to cancel. Take this function, with \(x\) in radians:

\[g(x)=\frac{\sin x}{x}\]

At \(x=0\) it is \(\frac{0}{0}\) again, and no factor removes the hole.

Nearby, \(g(\class{mjx-in}{0.1})=\class{mjx-out}{0.99833\ldots}\) and \(g(\class{mjx-in}{0.01})=\class{mjx-out}{0.99998\ldots}\).

To claim the limit is \(1\), set a challenge. Draw a band around \(1\), as thin as you like.

There must be a window around \(0\) where every input, other than \(0\) itself, gives an output inside the band.

A thinner band needs a narrower window, but a window can always be found.

That is exactly what a limit means. The band's half-width is called \(\varepsilon\) (epsilon), and the window's half-width \(\delta\) (delta).

\[\lim_{x\to \class{mjx-in}{a}} f(x)=\class{mjx-out}{L}\]

means that for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is a \(\delta>0\) such that

\[0<|x-\class{mjx-in}{a}|<\delta \;\implies\; |f(x)-\class{mjx-out}{L}|<\varepsilon\]

The \(0<\) on the left leaves out \(x=a\) itself. The hole is allowed.

Why Calculus Starts Here

Calculus studies change, and the rate of change at a single instant is a \(\frac{0}{0}\) problem.

The slope of \(x^2\) between \(x=1\) and \(x=1+h\) is the rise over the run:

\[\frac{(1+h)^2-1}{h}\]

At \(h=0\), this is \(\frac{0}{0}\): the slope between a point and itself.

For any other \(h\), it tidies up:

\[\frac{2h+h^2}{h}=2+h\]

As \(h\) closes in on \(0\), the slope closes in on \(2\).

\[\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(1+h)^2-1}{h}=2\]

That limit is the slope of \(x^2\) at the single point \(x=1\).

Every derivative is a limit of exactly this kind.

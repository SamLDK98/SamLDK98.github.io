The Power Rule

A Pattern

On The Derivative, finding a slope meant working out a limit. The answers follow a pattern, and the pattern can be explained.

Here are the slopes of the first three powers. The line \(x\) is its own tangent, \(x^2\) came from The Derivative, and \(x^3\) takes the same limit, worked out in the next section.

\[\begin{array}{c|ccc} f(x) & x & x^2 & x^3 \\ \hline f'(x) & 1 & 2x & 3x^2 \end{array}\]

Each time, the old power comes down in front, and the new power is one lower.

For any whole number \(n\),

\[f(x)=x^n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \class{mjx-out}{f'(x)}=nx^{n-1}\]

Even a constant fits. \(1\) is \(x^0\), and the rule brings down a \(0\): a flat graph has slope \(0\).

This is the power rule. But why should the power come down?

Why the Power Comes Down

Think of \(x^2\) as the area of a square with sides \(x\) long.

Grow each side by a small amount \(h\). The area grows by two strips, each \(x\) long and \(h\) thick, and a small corner square, \(h\) by \(h\).

In symbols, this is the square of a sum:

\[(x+h)^2=x^2+2xh+h^2\]

Divide the growth by \(h\): the strips give \(2x\), and the corner gives just \(h\).

As \(h\) closes in on \(0\), the corner's share vanishes and the strips' share stays. The area grows at \(2x\) per unit of side, and that is the slope.

Here the square has sides of \(1\), so the growth per unit of \(h\) is \(\class{mjx-out}{2+h}\): the slope Limits ended with. Its \(+\,h\) was the corner all along.

A cube works the same way. \(x^3\) is the volume of a cube with sides \(x\) long.

Grow each side by \(h\), and three faces push out. Each adds a slab \(x\) by \(x\) by \(h\). Along the edges between them sit three thin rods, and in the corner a tiny cube.

Below, the pieces are pulled apart so that each one shows.

\[(x+h)^3=x^3+3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3\]

Divided by \(h\), the slabs give \(3x^2\). The rods and the tiny cube still carry an \(h\), so they vanish.

Past three dimensions there is no picture, but the counting carries on. \((x+h)^n\) is \(n\) brackets multiplied together.

A term with exactly one \(h\) takes it from one bracket and \(x\) from all the others. There are \(n\) brackets to choose from, so there are \(n\) such terms, each \(x^{n-1}h\). For the square they were the two strips, and for the cube the three slabs.

That is where \(nx^{n-1}\) comes from. Every other term has at least \(h^2\). After dividing by \(h\) it still has an \(h\), so it vanishes as \(h\) closes in on \(0\).

Other Powers

The counting above only works for whole-number powers. Yet the rule holds for other powers too, wherever its formula gives a value.

Take \(\frac{1}{x}\), which is \(x^{-1}\). The rule predicts a slope of \(-1\cdot x^{-2}\), or \(-\frac{1}{x^2}\). The definition agrees:

\[\frac{\frac{1}{x+h}-\frac{1}{x}}{h}=\frac{x-(x+h)}{x(x+h)h}=\frac{-1}{x(x+h)}\]

As \(h\) closes in on \(0\), this closes in on \(-\frac{1}{x^2}\).

\(\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}\) has slope \(\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\), or \(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\).

\(\sqrt[3]{x}=x^{1/3}\) gets \(\frac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}\). That has no value at \(0\) and grows without end near it: the vertical tangent from The Derivative.

Proving the rule for every power needs more tools than this page has.

Multiples and Sums

Stretching a graph upward by \(5\) makes every rise \(5\) times bigger, while the runs stay the same. So every slope is \(5\) times bigger too.

That is why \(5t^2\) has slope \(5\cdot2t=10t\), the stone's speed on The Derivative.

Adding two functions adds their rises over the same run. So the slope of a sum is the sum of the slopes, and a polynomial's slope can be found one term at a time.

\[\begin{aligned} f(x)&=ax^2+bx+c\\ f'(x)&=2ax+b \end{aligned}\]

The \(c\) only lifts the graph, so it adds nothing to the slope.

Axis of Symmetry found the turning point of a quadratic where \(2ax+b=0\), by watching the sign of a small change. That expression is the slope: a parabola turns where its slope is \(0\).

A Stone Thrown Upward

Throw a stone straight up at about \(20\) metres per second, and \(t\) seconds later it is about \(20t-5t^2\) metres above your hand.

Term by term, its upward speed is \(\class{mjx-out}{20-10t}\) metres per second. A negative upward speed means it is falling.

At \(t=0\) it leaves at \(20\). The speed drops to \(0\) at \(t=2\), the top of the throw, about \(20\) metres up. By \(t=4\) it is back at your hand, falling at about \(20\).

The slope of \(20-10t\) is \(-10\), at every moment. Gravity takes about \(10\) metres per second off the upward speed every second, on the way up and on the way down.
